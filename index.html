02. nyt briefing

Good morning. The midterms were a historic collapse for the Republicans, who were unable to overcome an unpopular president and an unpopular war. We have the inside story.

And today, we explain what could come next.

by Adam B. Kushner and Evan Gorelick

As President Obama liked to say, elections have consequences. Tuesday’s contest — in which Democrats routed Republicans by shocking margins in shocking parts of the country — is no exception. How will Democrats use their new power?

The answer isn’t so simple. The result was more a rebuke of President Trump than an endorsement of specific policies, and Democrats disagree with one another on impeachment, the role of fossil fuels, Israel, health care and how much to tax the rich. Meanwhile, Trump still controls the executive branch — and the veto pen.

But the blue wave still promises to reshape American politics. Today, a guide to what’s coming.

The states

Democrats flipped at least five governorships and won vaunted “trifectas” — unified control of the executive and both legislative chambers — in at least five new states, cutting deep into territory once deemed purple and red.

A blue makeover. Their agendas, likely guided by the states that have been blue for a generation, will focus on how to steer funds and resist Trump’s policies. They could raise the minimum wage, lift taxes on top earners and add paid family leave. They may also pay to patch some of the cuts, particularly to food stamps and health programs, that the Republican Congress enacted as part of Trump’s domestic policy law. Some may try to restrict new A.I. data centers.

Electoral advantage. State Democrats will be empowered, in some cases, to change election rules — say, by expanding mail-in voting — in hopes of unlocking new swing states in the next presidential election. They may contemplate redrawing their congressional maps to help Democrats.

Washington

Congress. Chuck Schumer, the likely Senate majority leader, says job No. 1 is to bring down the cost of living for Americans. Democrats want to reverse Medicaid cuts, restore Obamacare subsidies, limit Trump’s ability to impose tariffs and curtail a war that has raised fuel costs. Trump promises he can be “Mr. Veto” if necessary. Even so, Democrats can force his allies into votes they’ll regret when the next election comes around.

Democrats also want to exercise their constitutional power to check the executive branch and conduct oversight. Now they promise to investigate crypto windfalls, well-timed stock trades and a wedding party for the president’s son bankrolled by a Kremlin-connected oligarch. One House Democrat said in a statement on Tuesday that White House officials should “lawyer up.”

The White House. Administration officials are preparing for the onslaught of investigations and discussing what they can do by executive fiat. But Trump is a lame duck with a conciliatory tone. He says he could work with the new congressional leaders to raise the debt ceiling or change immigration enforcement. “I’ll get along probably with the Democrats, unless they want to be destructive,” he said yesterday.

Two parties remade

Democrats. Tuesday’s result will send both moderates (such as Adam Hamilton of Kansas) and progressives (such as Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan) to Washington. The two factions fought bitterly in many primaries this fall, but candidates from both sides won Election Day victories. Progressives want the party to push bold, transformative ideas like Medicare for All. But Democrats can’t maintain their momentum in 2028 if they can’t persuade Trump voters to back them again. Which way will the party go? The argument between the camps will define the next two years.

Republicans. Trump pushes his allies to parrot his ideas — on war, government spending, the economy. He threatens apostates that he will back their opponents in primaries. But it hurt Republicans in this election to be seen as proxies for an unpopular president. Now that he’ll be a lame duck, Republicans may feel comfortable putting distance between themselves and Trump. It began yesterday, when Vice President JD Vance attributed the electoral drubbing to the war in Iran. After a defeat come the recriminations.
