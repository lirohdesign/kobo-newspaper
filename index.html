A largely stagnant synoptic pattern will allow for mild and dry conditions to continue likely through the end of October. Upper air observations reveal a large upper level ridge building over the central CONUS this morning; as it gradually continues to build, the ridge will shift eastward in the coming days. An Omega Block pattern is likely to setup with sinking air underneath the ridge yielding lots of sun, mild temperatures, and light winds. With an expansive area of surface high pressure also expected to anchor over the eastern US, this pattern will likely remain through the weekend into early next week. Expect several days of rinse and repeat weather with highs in the upper 60s to low 70s and lows in the low to mid 40s each day.

By Michael C. Bender

Cornell has a process for complaints like the one from Jane Doe, the pseudonymous former Cornell student who says seven men raped her at a frat party. All universities do. It’s called a Title IX investigation, and its purpose is to figure out whether and how much to punish the accused.

That might surprise people who know Title IX for something else — building up women’s sports in the United States. And now Trump’s vision for the law, too, has expanded beyond athletics and sexual misconduct. His administration is using it to prevent the government from recognizing genders besides men and women.

Title IX is just one provision in a 1972 education law. The provision was not the main concern of the lawmakers who passed the bill. President Richard Nixon, who enacted the measure, didn’t even mention Title IX when he signed the law.

Yet its enforcement has reshaped education — and also changed drastically based on the politics of the time.

A twisting history

The law took years to fully take shape.

Origins. Initially, Title IX forced universities to dismantle sex-based admission caps and expand women’s sports. Many credit the strength of U.S. women’s sports today to the pipeline created by this measure, which forced schools to spend the same amount on athletics for men and women.

A new view. In 1975, though, an instructor at Cornell, Lin Farley, coined the phrase “sexual harassment” to describe how female students and staff members were being fired, demoted or forced to quit because of unwanted advances from male bosses and co-workers. Soon, federal courts started allowing cases of sexual harassment under Title IX. For the next three decades, Republican and Democratic administrations told schools that Title IX banned sexual harassment.

Enforcement. But activists increasingly complained that federal oversight was so lax it jeopardized women’s access to education. So in 2011, the Obama administration told schools they needed a system to file complaints, an official to coordinate Title IX compliance and sexual harassment training programs. It also told schools to judge cases based on a “preponderance of evidence” (more likely than not that sexual harassment had occurred) instead of, say, “beyond a reasonable doubt” (the criminal standard).

The Education Department’s top civil rights lawyer, Catherine Lhamon, threatened to strip federal funding from schools that failed to adequately respond to sexual misconduct allegations. University officials told me she threatened to wield a “huge hammer” to enforce compliance. (I asked her about it: “That sounds exactly like me,” Lhamon replied.)

Backlash. At the same time, legal scholars, civil liberties groups and faculty associations warned that accused students were being punished without the due process they’d normally receive in the criminal justice system. From 2011 to 2020, students filed more than 700 lawsuits claiming universities had mishandled cases that accused them of sexual misconduct, according to a database maintained by an advocacy group that thought things had gone too far.

Reversal. Trump rescinded the guidance from the Obama administration, and his administration also rewrote — and codified — Title IX rules, which made them harder to change. The result was a 554-page addition to the Federal Register that provided more protections for the accused. It replaced administrative reviews with courtroom-style proceedings that required live hearings in which the accused and the accuser could see and hear each other. Lawyers for both sides could present evidence and cross-examine witnesses.

A court stopped President Joe Biden from overhauling the rules again.

A swinging pendulum

The broad language of Title IX has let successive presidents reinterpret its scope — and rewrite enforcement guidelines — with each shift in the cultural landscape, including the #MeToo movement and the aftermath. But college officials say the swinging pendulum of federal enforcement creates confusion on campuses, sowing doubt among students considering whether to report incidents.

That won’t change anytime soon. Trump is pulling back the Education Department’s focus away from sexual misconduct. Now it plans to use Title IX to formalize his order that male and female are the only sexes.

    QUOTE OF THE DAY

    John Geddes was a managing editor of The Times from 2003 to 2013 who helped oversee our transition from a print newspaper to a digital entity. He died this week at 74. Read his obituary, which among other things captures what John thought about The Times’s role in journalism, and what we’re all trying to do here. He told one of our editors:

    “I don’t think there can be a ‘paper of record.’ The term implies an omniscient chronicler of events, an arbiter that perfectly captures the significance and import of a day in our lives.”

    “I don’t work at that place,” Mr. Geddes continued. “I work at a newspaper that exists in a world where there are constraints of time, resources and knowledge. The wonder of the paper is that knowing the everyday limits to our ambitions doesn’t prevent us from trying to exceed them.”
