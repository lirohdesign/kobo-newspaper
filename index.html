02. nyt briefing

Good morning. Halloween presents an endless parade of ghouls and goblins. Most aren’t actually scary, but can illuminate some things about the nature of fear.

By Melissa Kirsch

My landlord’s spooky-season sidewalk tableau this year features three skeletons carrying an open casket, inside of which is yet another skeleton, as well as a disembodied hand. One morning last week, I emerged from the building to see two women with infants in strollers parked out front. It’s not unusual around Halloween to see families taking pictures of the spectacle, but I found it amusing that these people had stopped to show it to their babies. What would these tiny humans make of a surprisingly lifelike rotten-toothed crone beckoning them from the hedge? Would they recognize Halloween’s creepy cast of characters as frightening? Perhaps they were too young to even register what they were seeing. Perhaps the world in their eyes was still a series of shapes, light and color — everything new, everything still coming into focus, still taking on the valence of scary or safe.

The things that spook us as children leave their mark. We grow into adults who must disentangle real threats from perceived ones, and often discover that a present-day fear took root when we were too young to know better. Growing up, my family had a vinyl record of Boris Karloff narrating Prokofiev’s “Peter and the Wolf” that I found so frightening I could not look at the album cover. I insisted it be removed from my room and then lay awake worrying because the album was still somewhere in the house, pulsing with its fearsome power. Listening to a recording this week — No matter how hard the duck tried to run, she couldn’t escape the wolf. He was getting nearer, nearer, catching up with her — I felt a similar surge of terror as I felt as a child, combined with a pleasurable measure of gratitude that my more mature, rational brain can separate a ghost story from imminent menace.

In a recent Times article, the writer Alessandra Schade recalled that as a child she lived next door to the “Goosebumps” author R.L. Stine. She read his horror stories for kids and convinced herself that Stine’s house was haunted. Twenty years later, Schade met Stine, who told her about his career and how he came to inhabit the home from whose chimney she once was certain she saw ghosts floating. She learned that Stine and his wife, who met when they were editing magazines at Scholastic in the 1960s, had lived there, on a quiet cul-de-sac in Sag Harbor, on the eastern end of Long Island, for a quarter century. The source of her childhood anxiety turned out to be, as she put it, “a friendly octogenarian” who “goes by Bob.”

Our childhood fears resist rationalization. We can turn on the light and see that the boogeyman is just a pile of clothes, but we sleep with one eye open nonetheless. As adults, we have the tools to distinguish between real and imagined peril, but we don’t always use them. The scary album is replaced by the amorphous worry about a difficult conversation, a potential loss, things not turning out as we wish. We picture the worst-case scenario; we still see monsters where there’s just dirty laundry.

No matter how old we get, we still need to remind ourselves that what is unknown or mysterious to us isn’t necessarily dangerous.

Halloween is a laboratory for experimenting with this. All around us, bedsheet ghosts haunt the neighbor’s oak tree, spiders as big as hatchbacks climb up the side of tenements. And children, the ones most likely to shrink from this pageant of frights, walk through it delightedly, meeting witches and vampires and learning to distinguish costumes from reality.

    ADVICE FROM WIRECUTTER

    5 life-changing cleaning hacks

    By Christine Cyr Clisset

    I’ve learned all kinds of useful and unconventional hacks in my many years immersed in Wirecutter’s cleaning coverage. Here are five that have totally transformed my approach:

  • You can skip dry-cleaning plenty of things, even if the label insists you should.
  • Hand-washing many delicates — like wool or cashmere sweaters — can actually help them last longer.
  • Dish soap is an underrated cleaner, and works wonders on far more than just dishes. I use it to clean my oven, wood floors, appliances and even some laundry stains.
  • Make sure you’re cleaning the things that do the cleaning. For example, if your washing machine smells like mildew, it’s likely time to clean the filter.
