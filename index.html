As long as east winds continue Tuesday night/Wed AM and remain light, fog could occur as marine air is advected westward. A cold front approaches from the northwest Wednesday and may disrupt fog formation if clouds get to those areas early enough. This study of moisture content also begs the question about fire weather potential. Additionally, a cold front brings high clouds to the area Monday night limiting radiational cooling. Given these factors, have passed on issuing anything for frost Monday night. 02. nyt briefing

By Sam Sifton

Good morning. Peaceful antigovernment protests in France devolved into riots and looting. Now shopkeepers are picking up the pieces. And President Trump spent the weekend on the campaign trail, trying to make clear that the midterms are actually about him.

There’s a lot more below, including Julia Louis-Dreyfus’s Broadway debut. I’m going to start, though, with the human face of America’s utility bill crisis.

The economic news was grim last week. Government data revealed something millions of Americans have known for months: Rising energy costs, largely driven by the war with Iran, have pushed prices up all over. Wages are flat. And nearly half of Americans are having trouble paying for basic needs right now.

The climate news hasn’t been much better. It was the hottest summer on record in the United States, hotter than the Dust Bowl summer of 1936. Over the past few months, Americans ran a lot of air-conditioning to keep themselves cool — and, in some places, to keep themselves alive. Now about one in six U.S. households is behind on utility bills, according to federal data.

Some hot-weather states have laws to prevent utility companies from shutting off someone’s electricity during the most dangerous heat. But then comes fall, and the disconnections begin to mount. It’s when, Eli Saslow reports, “a climate crisis turns into a collection notice.”

Eli spent days with a couple and their family in the small city of McAlester, Okla., as they struggled to pay an electricity bill that was higher than their monthly income. When the heat index finally fell below 100 degrees at the end of September, the family had nine days to pay.

Here then were Genia Weitz and her husband, Daryl Mason. They have a three-bedroom house with two bathrooms but had installed themselves mostly in their 300-square-foot living room, hanging heavy blankets across the halls and doors to trap cool air from a rattly window-mounted air-conditioner. Weitz had been sleeping on the couch, with her oxygen machine plugged into the wall. Mason slept in the recliner with a CPAP machine for sleep apnea.

Weitz had just retrieved the disconnection notice from her mailbox. Eli captured the stakes:

“I can’t breathe if I don’t have my oxygen,” Weitz said, as she handed the bill to Mason. “I don’t think I’d last a single night.”

“We’d probably stroke out anyway in all this heat,” Mason said. “If we lose this room, we’re not going to make it.”

In crisis

Weitz and Mason’s power comes from the Public Service Company of Oklahoma, known as PSO. The company had told its customers that it faced rising costs itself. Demand for its electricity had risen because of the extreme weather — and because of all the juice required by new customers, including data centers.

The state has emerged as a hub for them. One proposed data center outside McAlester, Eli reported, “would use more electricity each week at full capacity than all the homes in McAlester used in an entire year.” PSO’s rates were below the national average, but the summer’s high temperatures had kept air-conditioners running around the clock. Bills were high all over. Weitz and Mason weren’t alone.

Weitz’s daughter-in-law went on social media and found hundreds of others in the same boat:

“My bill is unreal. I guess I’ll just burn up.”

“I use a flashlight on my phone before I turn a light on, and I pay $100 a week.”

“Single mom here. Cried on the phone with them last week because mine is so high.”

“My monthly bill is almost 900. I know it’s hot, but this is like an AC bill on Venus.”

A struggle

Eli was with Weitz as she tried to figure out what to do. The family’s car was broken down in the driveway, so she rode a shuttle bus into town to get a doctor to sign a form saying she had a serious medical condition. That was a chance to get 30 days of relief from disconnection. “Please be advised that a life-threatening situation does not cause the debt to be forgiven, but merely postponed to a later date,” the form read.

She got her monthlong reprieve — but not more money. Eli’s account of what happened next is extremely powerful.

    RECIPE OF THE DAY

    I love a veal piccata, a chicken piccata, even a swordfish piccata. Here’s a brilliant fourth option for that alchemy of lemon, capers and butter: tofu piccata. Press a firm brick of the stuff so it releases its water, cut it into slabs or cubes, coat it in cornstarch and pan-fry. Then build your piccata sauce while the tofu rests. You could nap it onto a bedroom slipper and have a good meal. (Make sure the butter you use at the end is cold, so it melts slowly into the sauce and emulsifies properly.)
