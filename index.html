.DISCUSSION... Issued at 305 PM EDT Tue Oct 13 2026

The short wave trough and pre-frontal forcing which aided in showers along and south of the US Route 24 corridor earlier today will continue to shift across the eastern Great Lakes region this evening. Isolated showers and thunderstorms have also developed during the mid afternoon across southwest Lower Michigan/far northern Indiana where some increase in low level frontogenesis forcing allowed development along weak confluence zone and also just downstream of what appears to be southwest extent of weak DPVA.

The focus for tonight into early Wednesday will be another low amplitude short wave trough ejecting out of the Dakotas and a resultant slow southward sinking baroclinic zone into Wednesday. Preceding this wave and frontal zone will be another surge of higher low level theta-e air. Isentropic forcing does not appear to be as pronounced as last night, but axis of fairly strong low level fgen forcing and increasing elevated instability should compensate for this with scattered showers and isolated/scattered thunderstorms expected to redevelop late evening into the overnight hours. Given elevated nature of the instability, severe weather is not expected. Some brief heavy rainfall is possible given increasing depth of moisture profiles, long/skinny CAPE profiles, and increasing warm cloud depths. Given quite dry antecedent conditions, significant hydro impacts are not expected.

By mid afternoon Wednesday, scattered shower/storm activity should progressively sink southward as low level fgen axis becomes more muted and shifted south. This will occur as some recovery in mid level heights occurs upstream in response to stronger upper trough/cut-off low feature lifting northeast out of the Four Corners region.

Another round of active weather is expected Thursday and Friday as the cut-off upper low begins to phase with a more dynamic northern stream upper level trough digging southeast out of south central Canada. This phasing system will likely result in cyclogenesis overhead late Thursday with some favorable upper jet dynamics also coming into play. This strongly forced setup should support another round of showers and thunderstorms Thursday afternoon and Thursday night before a strong accompanying cold front sweeps across the area. With the tendency to signals of better phasing, sfc reflection is likely to take a track closer to guidance that has been advertising a northerly track across IN/NW OH/SE Lower MI. This could provide a brief temporal window of weak near surface based CAPE across at least southeast portions of the forecast area late Thursday afternoon and more likely Thursday evening. Uncertainty remains with just how far north warm frontal feature can progress Thursday night but given robust kinematic profiles may need to monitor for isolated strong/severe threat Thursday evening, especially south of US 24. Given progression of features and dry antecedent conditions, widespread hydro concerns are not expected. PWATs will be highly anomalous on Thursday however, so some localized heavier rainfall amounts are possible.

Friday still appears dismal with blustery conditions developing on cold side of this deepening system. Peak afternoon gusts into the 35 to 40 mph range still appear possible given deep post-frontal mixing. Some consideration may be given to a wind advisory downwind of Lake Michigan, but uncertainty remains in this regard. Some lake effect rain showers will also be likely although duration of deeper lake induced instability profiles appears somewhat limited to Friday morning/early PM. Friday and Saturday will feature temps well below normal with nighttime lows in the 30s. Along with the better phased system comes the potential of a slightly quicker eastward progression of sfc anticyclone late Friday night with an attendant frost potential Friday night and Saturday night. Flow deamplification quickly ensues to start next work week which should result in temperatures moderating back to slightly above normal by Monday- Tuesday.

By Adam B. Kushner

I edit this newsletter.

The midterm elections are only a few weeks away, and readers of The Morning have sent us a lot of questions. Many of you wanted to know about President Trump’s taxpayer-funded ads, the prospect of ICE agents at polling stations and who’s going to win. (We’re not clairvoyant, but we do have helpful data.)

Times reporters have fanned out across the country, chronicling the highest-profile Senate contests that will decide who controls the chamber and the tiny municipal disputes about data centers. I dragooned five of them to answer your queries.

Why are candidates allowed to air ads that lie about their opponents? | Dawn Seggelink | Medford, Oregon

Courts give political speech the highest level of First Amendment protection. Candidates and PACs can be sued for defamation if they air damaging claims that they knew were false, or if they “acted with reckless disregard” of the truth. But the bar to win such a case is extremely high, and courts move slowly. More important, candidates don’t have to lie outright to fudge the truth. There is a well-established playbook for that, including by omitting context, airing unproven allegations and drawing misleading conclusions about unrelated facts. — Bayliss Wagner, who covers politics

Have pollsters made changes to get more accurate results? | Howard Frysinger | Glenmoore, Pennsylvania

Polling has transformed over the last decade or so. Pollsters now contact people over text message, on the phone, through the mail and online. For the Times/Siena poll, we have tried all of those different approaches to reach voters. In the end, we found the classic approach of calling voters on the phone still yields the best results. But we are constantly innovating and evaluating our approach to ensure we’re the most accurate. One change is in how we adjust our polls to make sure that they are representative. — Ruth Igielnik, a Times polling editor

Will ICE agents be at polling places? | Robert Halper | West Orange, New Jersey

Although some election officials and voting advocates worry that this prospect will drive away voters, they say the law is clear: Armed federal agents are not allowed in polling places. Administration officials have spread misinformation about the voter rolls, saying they bulge with noncitizens, but the Department of Homeland Security and the Department of Justice have said there are no plans to deploy armed agents to patrol polling places. (Government lawyers, on the other hand, are often on site.) — Alexandra Berzon, an investigative reporter who writes about election systems

How can my tax money be used for Trump’s political ads? | Judith Epstein | Overland Park, Kansas

Federal law prohibits using taxpayer money for “publicity or propaganda.” The White House lawyers blessed these ads as acceptable, but even some of the president’s allies are not convinced of that. The short answer is that federal dollars can be spent on public service announcements, but those usually are about a program or policy. These ads glorify the president; even one that is specific to an action the administration took — the capture of Nicolás Maduro — still included praise for the president. — Maggie Haberman, a White House correspondent

Why is voter ID controversial? We need an ID for many other aspects of life, so why not this one? | Mary Pat Sutter | Pewaukee, Wisconsin

Critics of voter ID laws have a few arguments. They go like this: The measures place an undue burden on eligible voters. Some laws restrict acceptable IDs to driver’s licenses, passports or state-issued IDs. Those cost money, require documents (copies of which you may need to pay for) and require time to obtain. Older, poorer and nonwhite voters are less likely to have those IDs. Many Democrats argue that one’s constitutional right to vote should not be limited by those factors, especially given how uncommon voter fraud is. — Michael Gold, who covers Congress
