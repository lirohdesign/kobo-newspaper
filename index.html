.DISCUSSION... Issued at 1247 PM EDT Fri Oct 23 2026

A largely stagnant synoptic pattern called an Omega Block will allow for mild and dry conditions to continue. Upper air observations depict a large upper level ridge continuing to build over the central CONUS this morning; as this feature gradually continues to amplify in the coming days, it will shift eastward over the Great Lakes. Sinking air underneath the upper level ridge will result our prolonged stretch of dry conditions, mild temperatures, and light winds continuing. High temperatures will be in the upper 60s to low 70s and lows in the low to mid 40s each day. With the Omega Block expected to break down by the middle of next week, there are some signals in long range model guidance that a pattern change is ahead by the end of the month into early November. Warmer than normal temperatures are likely to persist, however, a flip to more active, wetter weather is possible by late next week just in time for Halloween.

With the stagnant pattern locked in place, clear skies and light northeasterly flow off of Lake Erie will likely yield some patchy dense advection fog overnight into early Saturday morning. Best chances for fog will likely be northeast of Fort Wayne, IN as this is where the smallest dewpoint depressions and best moisture will be for fog to form. Much like the past few nights, the best timeframe for dense fog will be between 3 AM to 10 AM EDT Saturday, with the lowest visibilities around daybreak. A SPS or Dense Fog Advisory may be needed overnight depending on trends and where any fog develops.

02. nyt briefing

By Sam Sifton

Good morning. Happy Friday. Let’s start on our southern border.

You think Americans don’t like Flock cameras reading their license plates? The federal government has installed hundreds of powerful new A.I.-enabled cameras on tall towers all across the southern border of the United States, with plans to cover the nation’s perimeter, according to new reporting from Bora Erden and Nicholas Nehamas in The Times.

President Trump’s 2024 campaign soared partly on the popularity of his argument that too many immigrants had crossed the border illegally. Large majorities back tough enforcement there. And for decades, Republicans and Democrats have agreed that a high-tech “smart wall” would be a good and cost-effective way to stop people sneaking in.

That wall is rising now during an outcry about A.I. in general and mass surveillance in particular. It’s a stark illustration of a political axiom: Every policy choice comes with trade-offs. This one brings a potential loss of privacy.

The new technology can see for miles in all directions, tracking people and vehicles for 24 hours a day, without a human at the controls. A tireless A.I. algorithm does the work. Many of the cameras are in remote locations deep in the desert. But others are in more populated areas, where they can see into bedrooms, watch over public beaches, track children on playgrounds and transmit footage back to the authorities.

At least one million people in the United States and Mexico live in areas along the border where they can be seen by the surveillance towers, the Times analysis found. And at least four million more live under the gaze of an older generation of cameras that the government plans to retrofit with A.I. technology.

There are many more to come. The Trump administration wants them in Florida, Puerto Rico and the nation’s northern border. America has Ring cameras on its entryways now — and they can pick up a lot.

A security blanket

In the late 18th century, the philosopher Jeremy Bentham came up with the idea of the panopticon, a prison design that put guards in an all-seeing tower at its center. Panopticons have always made people uneasy. Modern thinkers came to view them as a metaphor for an omniscient watchfulness that would turn people docile and obedient. And some leaders are already wondering if the loss of privacy brought by these cameras is worth the gain in security.

“We don’t want to be surveilled by our government,” a Republican former city councilman in Texas told The Times. He urged members of the G.O.P. to imagine the towers under the control of Democrats.

To get a sense of what the cameras do, Times reporters went to two dozen A.I.-enabled camera towers in California, Arizona, New Mexico and Texas and launched a drone with a camera aboard it to approximate what the tower can see. And it’s a lot. The cameras can detect people coming in and out of houses. They can identify and track vehicles. They can follow people and cars from camera to camera as they move from one vantage point to another.

And they can alert agents of the Border Patrol, who can view the footage and decide whether to dispatch agents to intercept the targets. Those agents can also share the footage with local, state and federal law enforcement authorities, and even foreign governments.

“Any capability that improves an agent’s awareness and ability to assess potential risks helps protect agents, migrants and the public,” an official with U.S. Customs and Border Protection said in a statement.

The official from C.B.P. said the agency “employs technology while respecting civil liberties and privacy interests.” But there’s a catch. Its privacy guidelines say that people near the border can’t “opt out” of surveillance and affirm that the cameras could record people engaging “in First Amendment protected activities” or “complying with the law.”

The watched public

And what if people don’t know that the cameras are there, watching them? Most of the people The Times spoke to who live or work near the surveillance towers did not know their purpose, or their power. “Most thought they were cell towers or radar equipment,” my colleagues write.

Others shrugged. Surveillance is a way of life now. “This is the world we live in,” said a councilman in San Clemente, California, who supports the towers. “There are cameras everywhere. You walk down the street here, you’re being recorded. You can’t put the genie back in the bottle.”
