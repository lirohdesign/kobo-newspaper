.DISCUSSION... Issued at 317 PM EDT Wed Oct 14 2026

Surface cold frontal boundary has cleared southern portions of the forecast area this afternoon. The low level baroclinic zone is not expected to make much more additional southward progress into this evening as broad upper troughing across southern Ontario/Great Lakes region pushes east allowing some induced short wave upper ridging to build into the region downstream of larger scale cut- off low. Some isolated showers are possible through late afternoon/early evening with weakening frontal zone across the south, but otherwise prospects for additional rainfall tonight look to be on the low side given southward frontal push and break in stronger short wave forcing.

Water vapor this imagery this afternoon depicts two features that will be watched over the next 48 hours including a cut-off upper level low across SW CO and a more progressive vort max spilling down eastern side of western Canadian upper trough. Present indications still suggest a phasing of these features Thursday evening across the Corn Belt and Upper Midwest. Isentropic progs of 300-305K layer do indicate a rapid uptick in isentropic forcing toward late morning/midday. This evolution will allow the higher level theta-e air to return northward, particularly by Thursday evening. Showers with some embedded storms are expected to accompany this stronger advective push Thursday afternoon. Despite the strong advection, a strong low level inversion will develop in response and thus sensible warming at the surface likely to be more limited keeping highs in the 60s (possibly return of some low 70s late day/toward evening across the south). Severe weather is not expected during the day Thursday given weak mid/upper level forcing outside of the strong advective forcing and due to the elevated nature of the instability.

For Thursday night, the aforementioned upstream facing should begin to induce cyclogenesis of associated low pressure, however some subtle but important trends in guidance have been noted. A subtle trend of slightly more positive tilt with the upper trough appears to be suggesting sfc low deepening rates will be a little less impressive and more delayed than earlier indications. Robust shear profiles will develop ahead of primary low/sfc cold front but northward extent of a quality narrow warm sector remains uncertain, especially considering the trend of less rapid sfc feature deepening. There does still appear to be a narrow temporal window for weak near sfc instability across the south depending on extent of warm frontal progression, but hi res ensemble means still support best surface-based and mixed-layer CAPE remaining across central/southern IN. Only minor changes were made to SWODY2 with a Marginal Risk of severe storms persisting south of US 24, and mainly for the evening hours Thursday. Some isolated wind/tornado risk is possible where some weak MLCAPE can overspread given background low and deep layer shear, but current parameter space would tend to be more supportive of isolated severe threat southwest/south of local area from southern IL into central IN. Another limiting factor for severe potential could be more delayed impressive dynamics due to the weak positive tilt of this system. Otherwise, some brief locally heavy rain is possible with highly anomalous 1.75+ inch PWATs working into the area ahead of the overnight cold frontal passage.

Friday still appears to be blustery/windy day with strong cold advection on the back side of this system. This should lead to development of lake effect rain showers and a potential of mixing down some stronger wind gusts. With deepening rates of this system perhaps a bit weaker than earlier indications and some suggestion of a Great Lakes modification of secondary low level trough late in the day, guidance has backed off a bit on gust magnitude during the day, but would suspect some gusts to 30 to 35 mph would be possible. A few gusts approaching 40 mph appear most likely near immediate Lake Michigan shoreline. Given cold advection, temperatures are not likely to move much from morning levels with near steady temps in the 50s expected.

No major changes have been made to the long term forecast as below normal temperatures will continue through the weekend. Low temperatures in the 30s are still on track with a potential of patchy frost. The Friday night potential remains somewhat more uncertain as some lingering lake effect clouds could persist across the west, while winds may not full decouple across the east. Saturday night still appears to be best chance of patchy frost depending on extent of southwest return flow with departing high pressure.

Mid/upper level flow is still progged to rapidly deamplify early next week which will result in conditions warming to above normal levels into the lower 70s toward the end of the period.

02. nyt briefing

By Sam Sifton

Good morning. The pope has lung cancer, the Vatican said. Joni Mitchell is now an American citizen. And there’s more news below, including an accounting of how you get your bananas.

Let’s start, though, with Russia’s war on Ukraine.

With winter bearing down, Russia is trying to destroy Ukraine’s energy grid and freeze the country into submission after more than four years of war. It’s also bombing civilians. Ukraine has struck back with attacks on Russian oil refineries and, recently, e-commerce warehouses across the nation. It has clawed back some territory, even though it’s running low on munitions.

But as the price of diesel fuel soars, President Trump sees the United States as collateral damage in Ukraine’s fight for survival. “He wants to make problems for the world,” Trump said of President Volodymyr Zelensky. “He wants to make problems for our farmers, our ranchers.”

Accordingly, Trump announced on Friday that the United States would suspend sanctions on Russian diesel. The announcement shocked Ukrainian and European leaders who have worked for years, with the help of the United States, to cut Russia off from the money that fuels its war. (Several Republican members of Congress objected yesterday, too. Many of them had just backed a new law to tighten the economic noose on Vladimir Putin, the Russian president. “This deal is a gift to our adversary and a betrayal of our allies,” they and some Democratic colleagues wrote.)

But it surely pleased Putin. If the agreement moves ahead, it could put some $4 billion of American money into the Russian economy. In return, Putin offered more war, launching an attack on the Ukrainian city of Zaporizhzhia that killed 22 people, including four children.

The U.S. midterm elections are only weeks away, and high fuel prices imperil Republican candidates. Buying Russian diesel might bring those prices down, Trump suggests. It would be hard to find a clearer example of his “America first” posture toward foreign policy.

On the ground

Russia’s escalating assaults on Ukrainian cities are taking an enormous toll. The same week as the strike on Zaporizhzhia, a Russian bomb hit a commuter bus in Kramatorsk, killing at least 30 people.

The Russian military has targeted civilians throughout the war. But the death toll is rising as Moscow deploys more “precision weapons” that have stretched Ukraine’s air defenses with a daily shower of attacks. Russia launched about 3,500 jet-powered drones against targets across Ukraine in September, reports Marc Santora, who has been covering the war since its start. In June, that number was 350. Ukraine is trying to engineer an interceptor that can stop them. (For now, F-16s try to shoot them down.)

The Russians use older weapons, too, including World War II-era “glide bombs” that can be stopped only by shooting down the fighter jets that deliver them, something the Ukrainian military lacks the ability to do. In July, the Russians dropped 8,300 of those.

A father’s grief

Siobhán O’Grady and Liubov Sholudko, reporting from Ukraine, told the story of one of those bombs, which detonated in the city of Sumy, in the country’s northeast, not far from the Russian border. Their account opens with a horrifying scene:

The 500-pound Russian bomb first appeared as a tiny blip on Yevhen Ovcharov’s live military map: a red speck gliding over the Ukrainian border and above the villages beyond.

Then he saw it hurtling toward his hometown, Sumy.

Yevhen was a Ukrainian soldier deployed 300 miles from home, on the eastern front. It was 9:45 p.m. on July 3 when he reached for his phone.

His wife, Nataliia Ovcharova, answered quickly. Don’t worry, she told him. She and the girls were fine and happily walking home from the park. Their 13-year-old, Veronika, was trailing slightly behind with a friend. Their youngest, Milana, who was 5, was beside her.

At 9:46, the call was cut off.

Yevhen called back once, twice, three times.

For six minutes, he heard nothing.

At 9:52, his phone rang. It was Veronika. She screamed that she was covered in blood. Milana, she said, was lying completely still on the ground.

“No one is helping,” she sobbed. “Dad, please come save us.”

Yevhen ran outside and jumped in a car to start the four-hour drive home.
