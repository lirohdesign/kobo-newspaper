Good morning. New York has plunged fully into fall, complete with cozy scarves and puffy vests. Let’s go outside.

By Melissa Kirsch

Walking home late one night this week, I paused to register the perfect quiet, the air sweet with the scent of decaying leaves: the city in October, when the cold is still just flirting. The simple delight that Emily Brontë observed — “Every leaf speaks bliss to me / Fluttering from the autumn tree” — seemed attainable. What a waste to go inside and go to sleep when being out in the fresh night feels this good.

Fall is a season that rewards outdoorsy people, those who have been waiting patiently for the heat to break and the mosquitoes to retreat so they can layer on their wicking fabrics and take to the trails. The word “outdoorsy” is one that bedevils me: It’s one of those noun-turned-adjectives that purports to simply describe a person who likes to be in nature, but implies virtue. An outdoorsy person is healthy, active, connected to the earth. An indoorsy person, by contrast, is sedentary, vitamin D-deficient, screen-addicted. I aspire to all the moral characteristics of the outdoorsy set, and so have spent my adult life trying to become a person who can follow color-coded trail markers through the spruces.

Outdoor Boys, a YouTube channel with over 20 million subscribers, features people going to the furthest extremes of outdoorsiness: camping on frozen oceans, building survival shelters in blizzards, catching and cooking snakes. Natalie Berry, a freelancer, wrote about the phenomenon in The Times this week. The videos are “usually enjoyed indoors, by a predominantly male audience, who find comfort in watching others overcome discomfort,” she noted. They’re like housecats watching pumas and jaguars stalk jungle prey from the comfort of their living-room sun puddles.

Getting comfortable with discomfort seems to me to be the key to living a bigger, more fulfilling life. A few years ago, I had the rather rudimentary realization that my aversion to being cold was causing me to shrink my life to fit within the walls of my apartment during winter. I’d decline invitations, measuring the worthiness of an activity by its proximity to a thermostat. When I realized being cold is not an emergency — it’s just discomfort, and discomfort can be tolerated — I got a better winter coat and stopped dreading February so much.

Outdoorsiness as an identity, however, has continued to elude me. There’s a certain amount of skill that goes into erecting a tent, rappelling down a rock face. On message boards where people ask how they can become more outdoorsy, the advice is to start slow: Go to a park and build an evening campfire before planning a weeklong camping trip. Those of us who aspire to outdoorsy might embrace the alternate descriptor outsidey in the meantime. “Outdoorsy is nature for adventure. Outsidey is nature for leisure,” goes a TikTok on the distinction. Mountaineering is for the outdoorsy, it explains, whereas an outsidey person will “frolic upon the meadow.” It’s a lighthearted differentiation, but the ultimate effect is one of inclusiveness. You don’t have to summit a mountain in order to appreciate the foliage. You don’t have to be athletic or intrepid in order to be connected to the natural world.

    RECIPE OF THE WEEK

    By Melissa Clark

    Roasted Red Pepper and Feta Cornbread

    There are so many ways to bake a batch of cornbread. Sweet or savory, firm or fluffy, plain or loaded with aromatics and cheese: You really can’t go wrong. Lidey Heuck adds roasted red pepper and feta to her easy cornbread recipe, giving the loaf pockets of tangy cheesiness. The sour cream in the batter means this won’t dry out if you want to make it a day ahead — that is, if you can resist a pan of warm cornbread cooling on your counter. I can’t.
