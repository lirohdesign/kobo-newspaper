Good morning. The New York City Marathon is this morning.

I’m the fitness editor and a compulsive runner.

A marathon is a beautiful, painful thing.

Runners young and old, fast and slow, find out how far they can push themselves after months of preparation and nerves. Some will cross the finish line elated; others won’t make it. Runners often say that “the marathon owes you nothing,” because it can punish even the well-trained. When I ran my first one, I hit the wall at Mile 16 (but slogged to the finish).

I’ve run 12 marathons in the last decade, and a lot has changed in that time. Take the New York City Marathon, the joyful Sunday when people across the five boroughs cheer, make funny signs and gawk at the sufferfest. About 60,000 people will start today’s race; a few hundred thousand more applied for a lottery spot and didn’t get in. Why are so many people seeking bibs?

Marathons are suddenly very popular: Many more people are vying for spots in the biggest races. Meanwhile, both elite and recreational runners keep getting faster. Here’s what to know about how the marathon is evolving.

A running boom

Interest in running has surged in the past few years, and major marathons have seen big jumps in demand: 1.3 million people applied to run next year’s London Marathon, up from 578,000 just three years ago. New York saw a nearly 20 percent increase in lottery entries this year over last year. Only a small fraction were selected to run. (Some racers get in through other avenues, like fast qualifying times.)

Many people picked up running, or returned to it, during the pandemic. Since then, run clubs have contributed to the boom. The groups are particularly popular among younger people, and plenty have found that sweating side-by-side can lead to fast friendships, or even dates. These folks are also signing up for more races.

As the marquee races become harder to enter, runners have found alternate ways to challenge themselves: In New York, some also-rans circumnavigate Manhattan as a D.I.Y. 32-mile ultramarathon.

Peak performance

At the front of the pack, there have also been a few big shifts.

Until very recently, many people thought a sub-two-hour marathon wasn’t possible. This spring at the London Marathon, runners broke that barrier for the first time in an official race — and it wasn’t only the winner. The second-place finisher did it, too.

Why are top runners getting so much faster? Here are a few reasons.

The shoes: Sneaker technology has evolved. About 10 years ago, Nike introduced the Vaporfly, a running shoe with thick cushioning and a carbon plate designed to improve a runner’s efficiency. The footwear had a significant effect on performance. Today, most major running brands offer their own “super shoes,” as they’re known. (The record breakers in London were wearing a version from Adidas.) While the earliest super shoes were worn mainly by elites, they’ve trickled down to recreational runners too. I use a pair that costs nearly $300.

Extra carbs: When you’re running for hours, you need carbohydrates to fuel your muscles. Most runners get the quick energy they need in the form of gels and sports drinks along the course. But research suggests that trained athletes can digest more carbs — and thus perform better — than we thought. So top endurance athletes now gulp a lot more of that goop during the race.

I’ve tried that, too. It’s not always gentle on the stomach, but it can make those final miles less of a slog.

    Who pays for President Trump and Vice President Vance’s travel to stump for candidates? | Victoria Garvin | Washington, D.C.

    The short answer: It’s a mix. The government covers the cost of protecting them, including Secret Service operations. And the taxpayer foots the entire bill for air travel at first. But campaigns or political action committees must reimburse other aspects of these trips, such as food and venue rentals. In the case of political events, usually the Republican National Committee sends the check. I asked the White House and the R.N.C. how much they’ve spent so far. They didn’t answer. It’s common for the administration to take months to submit a bill to the party for its share of the costs. — Luke Broadwater, White House correspondent

    BOOK OF THE WEEK

    By Elisabeth Egan

    “Miss Wolcott’s Ghost,” by Louise Penny

    You don’t need to be on a first-name basis with Chief Inspector Armand Gamache, Penny’s French Canadian equivalent of Hercule Poirot, to appreciate this thoughtful, moving whodunit. The case begins with a mysterious phone call announcing a dead body in Lost Nation. But where is Lost Nation, exactly? And why does the call trace back to Gamache’s neighbor, who denies placing it? “The ‘how,’ even the ‘who’ of a murder investigation, was important,” Penny writes, “but never as interesting or telling as the ‘why’s.” As in previous Gamache novels, Penny tackles all three with alacrity.
