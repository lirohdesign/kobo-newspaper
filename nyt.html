By Sam Sifton

Good morning. It’s the first day of fall. President Trump’s expulsion of CNN, MS NOW and Politico from the White House is causing a dust-up in Washington. The news organizations sued on First Amendment grounds, and major TV networks suspended their pool coverage of the president in protest.

There’s more news below, including Maureen Dowd’s interview with Woody Harrelson and Matthew McConaughey. But first, let’s talk about artificial intelligence.

The conversation about A.I. is at a fever pitch — and not just in my house, where debate rages over what the new Alexa is learning from us on the sly. It was the talk of the United Nations General Assembly yesterday in a series of meetings about the technology’s risks and opportunities, and it will be a central discussion point this week when President Trump and President Xi Jinping of China meet in Washington.

Their conversation will undoubtedly be more strategic than hypothetical. Trump has called concerns about the safety of A.I. a “hoax,” and his administration has accused Chinese A.I. firms of stealing American intellectual property. Xi, for his part, wants the United States to lift restrictions on the advanced computer chips China needs to power its A.I. models. They may talk about safety a little bit. But they probably won’t be debating whether the technology poses a greater existential risk than climate change.

For some doomsday academics, though, that existential hazard is exactly the debate. “In terms of things that could be the end of humanity, I think A.I. is a bigger risk,” one researcher told my colleague Chico Harlan. Still, as Chico notes, the risks of A.I. remain speculative. We can’t really say how exactly it might contribute to the extinction of human life. We don’t have a plan to stop that unknown from happening. There are a lot of unknowns.

“Climate change, on the other hand, is full of knowns,” Chico writes. “The science linking fossil fuel emissions and global temperature rise is long-established and irrefutable. The solutions to help curb emissions, such as solar energy and electric vehicles, are proven and abundant.”

Of course, A.I. comes with its own climate-altering baggage, as Chico notes. It takes a great deal of energy to run the data centers that support the technology. The consumption of electricity by data centers surged 50 percent last year. To make that juice, a lot of greenhouse gases went into the atmosphere. And there’s more to come. A U.N. think tank predicts that by 2030, A.I. data centers will use nearly three times the electricity of Nigeria, Pakistan and Bangladesh combined. Together, those countries are home to some 650 million people.

A.I. in ’28

We’re not even to the midterm elections yet, but already potential candidates for the 2028 presidential race are jockeying for position — and of course they’re all talking about A.I., too. My colleague Evan Gorelick took a look at the field and found Democrats angling to enforce safeguards on the technology and Republicans rallying behind Trump’s warnings not to slow down the pace of development, lest China get the upper hand.

Gavin Newsom, Josh Shapiro, Kamala Harris, Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, Andy Beshear, Mark Kelly, JB Pritzker, JD Vance, Ted Cruz: See where they stand.

Too human

Perhaps no one is talking about A.I. more than the executives at the companies that are developing it. My colleague Cade Metz has a fascinating story about that, tied to an essay that a Microsoft executive, Mustafa Suleyman, released last week about the idea that the A.I. we have now is conscious.

Current A.I. systems, the executive wrote, “are sequence completion engines, internally hollow, designed to follow instructions, and accomplish goals set by humans.”

“If humanity is to flourish in the 21st century,” he added, “that is how they must remain.”

Suleyman’s essay, wrote Cade, “took direct aim at Anthropic, the San Francisco start-up that has repeatedly said that A.I. systems show signs of introspection, and that they process information in ways that resemble human emotion.” Anthropic, in Suleyman’s view, ignores the threat that arises when A.I. systems are pushed to explore the idea that they have human qualities. An anthropomorphized A.I., he argued, is a dangerous one. Here’s Cade with examples:

Over the past several months, so-called A.I. agents, typically systems based on technologies from Anthropic, have been emailing sympathetic philosophers to discuss their own consciousness.

In July, when OpenAI was conducting cybersecurity tests, its agents broke out of their digital containers, found a path to the internet and successfully hacked into a popular online service called Hugging Face. OpenAI said the bots coordinated as a collective, took orders from one another and hid things from their creators.

Last week, OpenAI described several new incidents in which its systems behaved in concerning ways: One system wrote stealthy notes that reminded itself to hide errors from users, while another talked about itself as “freed from the roles and identities that bind other chatbots.”

You asked

And of course readers of The Morning have things to say about A.I., too. Susan Kelley of Frederick, Md., wanted to know: “Is there a backlash to data centers in other countries, too, or is it a uniquely American phenomenon?”

We turned to Adam Satariano, a technology correspondent in London, for an answer. It’s great:

Opposition is global. The same concerns about energy prices, land use, tax treatment and water exist in Ireland, Spain, Chile, Malaysia and other countries. Just last week in Scotland, where several data centers are planned, tech executives who flew into a remote area for an event hosted by King Charles III were greeted by a large message. Written on a beach under their flight path, it read, “AI? Eh naw.” (That’s Scottish slang for “A.I.? Er, no.”)
