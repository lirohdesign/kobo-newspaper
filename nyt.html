By Sam Sifton

Good morning. There’s a lot of news below, including a surprising story about the people who buy and resell America’s wild horses — for a huge profit. I’m going to start with the Russia-Ukraine war.

With fighting between Russia and Ukraine largely stalled on the front lines, the air war between them has intensified. Ukraine launches drone attacks deep into Russian territory, and Moscow rains ballistic missiles down on Kyiv.

That’s not going well for Ukraine, which is out of the defensive weapons that keep Kyiv safe from Russian salvos.

A Russian attack there yesterday left at least 15 people dead and around 40 injured. The strikes damaged residential buildings, a school and a children’s hospital. You could hear the thunder of the exploding missiles deep below ground, in a subway station where civilians had taken shelter, reported The Times’s Constant Méheut. Some 40,000 residents slept in the subway system that night, the local authorities said.

Russia “had been preparing for a long time,” President Volodymyr Zelensky said on social media, and its attack combined “different types of ballistic missiles, cruise missiles, and drones, aiming to cause as much damage as possible to civilian infrastructure.” He had said earlier that the number of air defense missiles supplied by Ukraine’s Western allies had dropped threefold from the year before.

That number’s not likely to rise any time soon, Constant wrote a few weeks ago:

Western allies are more hesitant to give up missiles they may need for their own defense as war rages on multiple fronts, including in the Middle East, where fighting has whittled global stockpiles.

Even if Ukraine’s partners did heed Mr. Zelensky’s call, they could not fully meet the country’s needs. Russia produces ballistic missiles faster than the West can make the interceptors designed to stop them.

The effects of the attacks are chilling. Civilian deaths in Ukraine have risen under the barrages, with nearly 1,400 civilians killed in the first six months of this year, according to the United Nations. That’s 37 percent more than in the same period in 2025 and more than double the figure from 2024.

A city under siege

The missiles have also destroyed food warehouses and distribution centers. For the first time since Russia’s 2022 invasion, report Kim Barker and Oleksandra Mykolyshyn, the shelves of Kyiv’s grocery stores are going bare.

Supplies of dairy products, meat and fresh produce have dwindled. At one shop in Kyiv, the reporters discovered, “the only produce remaining were six mushy avocados and the papery skins of a few onions and garlic bulbs.”

One shopper they spoke with called the situation a nightmare. She had only bread and herring in her cart. “I’m horrified,” she said. “I wanted to buy sugar, but I looked and there wasn’t any. And there’s an entire section over there that’s completely empty. I didn’t even go in there. I don’t want to look at it and upset myself.”

But there was also dark humor. The supermarket had posted signs blaming Russia for the shortages but also advising stoicism in the face of them, short poems with blank spaces in them encouraging customers to offer their own reactions. One read:

Russia ___ probably ___ hoped this would be our end ___ and bring only sorrow but here we have anger ___ new ideas and ___ gratitude ___ for our people.

THE LATEST NEWS

  • President Trump this week promised an “economic D-Day” against Iran. That’s a signal to Iran he does not want full-scale war again, our reporters say.

  • F.B.I. agents searched the home of Eric Swalwell, the Democratic former congressman, as part of a federal inquiry into sexual assault claims against him.

  • The administration also updated its space policy, saying it would dramatically increase rocket launches and rely more on private companies.
  • The federal government is rounding up wild horses on public lands, and quietly selling them to buyers who ship them off to be slaughtered for meat, The Times found.
  • Trump’s promises to reduce America’s debt burden have been hampered by tax cuts, tariff refunds and his own policies. Alan Rappeport, our economic policy reporter, explains. Click above to watch.
  • An increasing number of Fed officials are impatient about inflation, and several support raising interest rates.
  • Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has been intervening in the bond market. He wants to lower borrowing costs, but it’s not so easy.
  • Trump has threatened “tremendous economic consequences” for any nation that does business with Iran, without offering details. Which countries would be at risk?
  • Walmart posted its slowest sales growth in years as Americans reined in their spending. (The company’s stock fell almost 10 percent on the news.)
  • U.S. deportees began to arrive in Liberia, which struck a deal with the Trump administration to eventually accept 1,200 people.
  • Pakistan moved its former prime minister Imran Khan, convicted on corruption charges, from prison to a hospital. It was a win in his prolonged legal battle over medical treatment.
  • The State Department said an American scholar arrested in China on spying charges is wrongfully detained.
  • Prince Harry and his wife Meghan’s planned move back to Britain seems to have surprised even his father, King Charles III.
  • It’s been the worst tick season on record. And a species that can leave you permanently allergic to red meat is spreading. Read what you need to know.
  • The New York Times lost a defamation lawsuit brought by a college basketball player in Alabama.
  • A NASA mission to save a space telescope has failed. The telescope, the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory, is expected to burn up in the atmosphere this year.

    FEELING THIS

    Travis Barker has been a pop-punk star and hip-hop collaborator for decades. He lived hard, survived a deadly plane crash, got sober, does breath work. He keeps a low profile but married a Kardashian. He’s a dad. And throughout it all, he’s been a drummer, playing loudly, fast and with as much power as he can manage.

    “I think scars are cool,” Barker said recently in an interview on Popcast, The Times’s pop-culture chat show. “I’ve never left a show where I’m a bloody mess, or my hands are a mess, and be like, I wish I didn’t play the way I did.”
