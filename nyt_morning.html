Our final polls

By Shane Goldmacher

The fate of President Trump’s final two years in the White House is on the line. Republicans have controlled all of Washington since Trump’s second inauguration. But voters across America will decide tomorrow whether to hand Congress to Democrats.

The real fight is for the Senate. And in our final polls of the cycle, they have the edge in six of seven battleground states. They even lead in Texas and Kansas — voters in Kansas have not sent a Democrat to the Senate since 1932.

Here’s what else to know about the race.

Congress

This midterm election — like most midterms — appears to be mostly a referendum on the president. Trump’s popularity is at all-time lows. How much does any aversion to him adhere to the rest of his party? That’s the question voters will answer tomorrow.

The House. Polls show that Democrats are favored to win control of the lower chamber. This isn’t a big surprise. The party out of power almost always gains seats in a midterm, and Democrats need to flip just a few seats for a majority.

The Senate. Right now, Republicans have 53 seats in the upper chamber. Democrats need to flip four states to take the majority because Vice President JD Vance would break a potential 50-50 tie. Two years ago, Democrats seemed unlikely to snag control; the states up for grabs this year so clearly favor Republicans. Yet the deteriorating political climate for Republicans and strong Democratic candidates have given the party a chance in deep traditional Republican territory.

Much of the battle is in places like Alaska, Kansas, Texas, Ohio and Iowa — states that Trump has won every time he has been on the ballot, some by landslide margins. But the Times/Siena polls yesterday show why Democrats have a chance. Just 38 percent of likely voters approved of the president’s job performance in the seven states that were polled. And Democrats have a nine-point advantage on the year’s most pressing issue: the cost of living.

The glimmer of good news for the G.O.P. is that late-breaking voters are trending their way: Voters who decided only in the last month favor Republicans by a nine-point margin.

It’s worth remembering, though, that surveys are not predictions. (As Nate Cohn, our chief political analyst, puts it in his column: “The final polls show a blue surge. Can we trust them?”) Strategists from both parties say a wide range of outcomes are possible. Republicans have only really surrendered on one single seat (in North Carolina), and they are still trying to capture Michigan, where the Democratic nominee is an outspoken progressive who has spent recent days feuding with his Democratic former primary rival, who endorsed the Republican in dramatic fashion yesterday.

Whose advantage?

In many ways, the midterms boil down to a contest of Democratic momentum versus a Republican map and Republican money.

Trump raised nearly $450 million into his super PAC, and Elon Musk, the world’s richest man, has contributed more than $400 million to help federal Republicans. Democrats have vastly out-raised Republicans in small online donations. But the party does not have a financial answer to those huge sums.

In race after race, Democrats are making the case that Republicans did not follow through on the central promise of Trump’s 2024 campaign: lowering the cost of living. Inflation is up broadly, and voters are particularly aggrieved about gas prices that soared as a result of the Iran war.

Perhaps the biggest difference between this year and Trump’s first midterm in 2018 is that the president’s traditional base of white voters without college degrees has lost faith in his stewardship of the economy. It’s one of the reasons the bottom could fall out from under his party tomorrow in traditional red states.

Both sides are spending at levels befitting the stakes. A record-setting more than $11 billion has been spent on advertising in the midterms.

The aftermath

What happens after the vote is counted? Just winning the House alone would be enough for Democrats to stall Trump’s legislative agenda for the rest of his presidency. It would also hand Democratic congressional officials subpoena power to investigate the president, his administration and his allies.

But claiming the Senate would give Democrats a functional veto power over his judicial appointments — including to the Supreme Court — and his effort to fill many top administration jobs, including in the cabinet.

ASK THE MORNING

In today’s very polarized political climate, is campaigning about getting people to switch sides? Or is it more about increasing voter turnout? Or both? | Ruth Bieber-Stanley | Cleveland Heights, Ohio

You’re asking a question that every campaign has to consider, and the answer depends where they’re running. Both sides are focused on firing up their bases. That’s especially the case for Republicans, and it’s why President Trump has embarked on a red-state tour in recent weeks. The theory was that Republicans can win if Trump’s devoted supporters show up. But in those same places, Democrats are trying to reach voters in the middle — independents and moderate Republicans. Adam Hamilton, the Democratic pastor running for Senate in Kansas, is an example of this approach. He’s trying to persuade Kansans that he cares about the same issues they do, no matter if they belong to the other party. — Jess Bidgood, who writes the On Politics newsletter.

More on culture

  • Top-flight classical musicians benefit from playing top-flight instruments. The trouble is, those instruments can cost millions of dollars. Rich people step in with open-ended loans and “musical mortgages” to make it possible. They’re not just altruistic. There’s a return on the investment.
