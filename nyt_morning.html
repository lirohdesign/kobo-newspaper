A new voting restriction

A ballot processing center. Philip Cheung for The New York Times

The Constitution gives the White House no power over elections, and Congress has declined to pass a voter I.D. measure that President Trump wants. This past spring, he found a workaround: He told the government to build a nationwide list of eligible voters that the Postal Service could use to prevent noncitizens from voting by mail.

Yesterday, the Supreme Court said the president can restrict mail-in voting — at least for now, while the courts hear arguments for and against it.

Feasibility. The Department of Homeland Security is supposed to create “state citizenship lists” of people who will be 18 or older on Election Day. Those names go to state election officials, who administer the vote, purportedly so they can purge anyone who is not a citizen from voter rolls. Then postal workers block ballot requests from anybody not on the lists. It’s a complex endeavor, considering the midterm elections are two months away and the Supreme Court justices may still hear full arguments. Critics point out that voter fraud is exceptionally rare, and that the new system is more likely to disqualify legitimate voters than impede foreign cheaters.

Legality. Several states have sued, saying the effort could disenfranchise voters and cause chaos in the midterms. But in an unsigned emergency order, the Supreme Court said that those states could not demonstrate they were sufficiently harmed by voting rules that had not yet taken effect.

American gladiators

The degenerative brain disease known as C.T.E. stalks professional football players. We’ve seen it in the headlines for years, usually attached to a famous name. Hall of Famers like Junior Seau and Frank Gifford had C.T.E. So did the convicted murderer Aaron Hernandez, who killed himself in prison. Repeated hits to the head can cause chronic traumatic encephalopathy, and it can be diagnosed only by examining the brain after death.

An alarming study released today suggests that C.T.E. is not just lurking at the margins, an outlier outcome for the most risk-prone players — in fact, at least 25 percent of N.F.L. players might expect to have it.

John Branch, a reporter for The Times, wrote about the study. He has covered C.T.E. in professional sports for nearly 15 years. Researchers, he tells us, looked at the 878 former N.F.L. players who died from 2016 to 2021. They were able to test the brains of 235 of them for C.T.E. Of those, 215 had the disease. That’s 24.5 percent of the total. “The actual prevalence could be far higher,” John writes. “The roughly 25 percent rate does not consider the 643 brains that were not examined. Some of those, maybe many of them, likely had C.T.E. too.”

John then applied the statistics to today’s N.F.L. players. When the regular season starts in a couple of weeks, there will be 1,696 people on the league’s 32 active rosters. (That’s not including hundreds on practice squads or out for injuries.) At least 400 of them, John reports, will develop C.T.E., if the rate continues.

That’s a terrifying number. Symptoms of the disease include memory loss and confusion, as well as dramatic mood swings. Executive functioning decreases. Erratic behavior increases, as does addiction. Suicides happen. (Other studies have shown that the rate of C.T.E. in people who haven’t gotten hit in the head a lot is nearly zero.)

Zack Wittman for The New York Times

John’s sentences about all this are spare, relentless. Here he is on the San Diego Chargers’ 2009 roster, which included at least three players who were diagnosed posthumously with C.T.E.:

One was Vincent Jackson, a three-time Pro Bowl receiver who died in a hotel room of alcohol poisoning at 38. Another was Kevin Ellison, a defensive back who died at 31 when he was struck by a car after wandering onto a California freeway.

The other was defensive back Paul Oliver, who was 29 in 2013 when he shot himself on the stairs of his family home in front of his wife, Chelsea, and their two young sons.

Where’s OSHA?

Imagine having a job like that, where 25 percent of your colleagues end up with a debilitating disease that will kill them, but not before making their lives and the lives of their loved ones miserable. You’d probably be safer as a commercial fisherman, or a coal miner. One scientist told John that Russian roulette has better odds. “We never see things that are this dire,” he said.

Wouldn’t the existence of such a job trigger outrage and calls for change from, among others, the Occupational Safety and Health Administration?

John wrote about that, too, today. His answer: Not if your job is playing professional football. The agency told him: “In most cases, OSHA does not take enforcement action with regard to professional athletes.”

The N.F.L. is simply too big and too popular for people in government to want to change its rules or abolish the sport. In 2024, its revenues were more than $23 billion, and 35 of the top 50 U.S. telecasts last year were N.F.L. games. Dangerous as it is, football is our culture.

And there’s also this, which John told me yesterday. “C.T.E. is one of those things that most people take seriously only when it directly affects them, like gun violence or climate change,” he said. “Until then, it can feel like someone else’s problem.”

RECIPE OF THE DAY

Christopher Simpson for The New York Times. Food Stylist: Simon Andrews.

Eric Kim’s recipe for gochujang-glazed eggplant with fried scallions is a loose adaptation of one for gaji bokkeum, a Korean dish of stir-fried eggplant. The gochujang provides sweetness and heat, and the scallion oil he uses to cook the eggplant brings an allium brightness. All the flavors are present. Spoon over white rice and enjoy.
