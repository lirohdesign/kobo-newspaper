Good morning. Votes are still trickling in for one of the biggest primary races of the year, the Democratic contest for a Senate seat in Michigan. We’ll start there.

Dr. Abdul El-Sayed and Representative Haley Stevens. Nick Hagen for The New York Times; Finn Gomez/Getty Images

Still counting

A marquee Senate race in Michigan, which Democrats are watching to gauge the strength of a left-wing insurgence that has swept through primary elections this year, remains too close to call this morning.

Dr. Abdul El-Sayed, a progressive former public health official, leads Representative Haley Stevens by just over one percentage point, with more than 95 percent of votes counted in the state. But the largest share of uncounted votes is in Wayne County, which includes Detroit, where Stevens has held an edge. See live results here.

Recent polls had forecast a comfortable victory for El-Sayed, who promised to end U.S. military aid to Israel and attacked Stevens repeatedly for taking at least $30 million from AIPAC, a pro-Israel lobbying group. The race wound up a lot tighter.

“We knew this was going to be a close race, and that’s exactly what we’ve got,” Stevens told supporters shortly before 1 a.m.

Left-wing candidates have won plenty of primaries this year, despite seemingly endless Democratic hand-wringing about their ability to court mainstream voters come November. But their victories have been largely confined to highly educated and heavily blue areas in urban America. El-Sayed wants to break that trend.

At least one progressive candidate scored a decisive win last night: Down ballot in Michigan, the left-wing organizer William Lawrence prevailed in one of the most competitive House districts in the country after his two centrist opponents, each refusing to drop out of the race, split the moderate vote. When I interviewed Lawrence over the weekend, he told me it was his priority “to stop these stupid wars around the world that are wasting trillions of our dollars, including for war crimes committed by Israel.”

BUGGING OUT

A small insect trapped in a Saxifraga candelabrum. Xin-Jian Zhang

Everyone needs a hobby. For Charles Darwin, the father of evolutionary biology, it was carnivorous plants. He obsessed over them, studied their features and wrote about them with obvious excitement. He once remarked that he cared more about sundews — plants that lure insects into gluey traps and eat them — “than the origin of all the species in the world.”

Can you blame him? I can’t. I love carnivorous plants, too. When I was 10 or 11, I spent months begging my parents for a Venus fly trap. (They wouldn’t budge, so I settled for a mini red cactus I found by the checkout line at Home Depot.)

In 1875, Darwin theorized that alpine plants with sticky hairs reminiscent of his beloved sundews, in the genus Saxifraga, could be flesh-eating. He tempted two species with insects but ultimately couldn’t prove they were carnivores.

Now, more than 150 years later, scientists have vindicated Darwin’s hypothesis. A team of researchers served tiny flies to a Saxifraga species in China and found that the plants could digest and absorb nutrients from their prey. A botanist at Michigan State University said there could be hundreds, even thousands, of other species with similarly sticky hairs, including the common tomato. Some of them may be secretly carnivorous.

A lot of one-pot pastas come out dry or gooey. Not Alexa Weibel’s one-pot zucchini-basil pasta.
