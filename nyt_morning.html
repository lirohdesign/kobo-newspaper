Dark arts

President Trump wants to leave his mark on Washington, D.C., sometimes literally. He’s building a huge new ballroom and bunker complex in the White House. He has a shiny new helipad out back. He talks about his huge triumphal arch for Memorial Circle, which would also be a sniper hide and place to launch drones. And the U.S. Institute of Peace is now the Donald J. Trump United States Institute of Peace.

But perhaps no place in the nation’s capital reflects Trump’s insistence on approval and recognition more than the John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts, the nation’s unofficial national theater, its flagship stage. Trump wants his name in lights there. “We’re going to make it hot,” he said at the start of his second term in office. “We made the presidency hot, so this should be easy.”

My colleagues Elizabeth Williamson and Julia Jacobs have chronicled Trump’s odyssey with the Kennedy Center. It’s a drama that has unfolded over seven acts. Let’s look at them.

Pique. After Trump won his first presidential election in 2016, some of the recipients for the next year’s Kennedy Center Honors — the institution’s annual awards for artistic achievement — said they’d boycott a White House reception. By tradition, the president hosted that reception before an evening of performances at the Kennedy Center. Trump’s response was to cancel the reception and avoid the show. He stayed away for the rest of his first term.

Takeover. When Trump returned to office last year, he was set on remaking the government — and the nation’s cultural life, which he said had become too woke. That included the Kennedy Center. He quickly fired the Biden-era appointees on the center’s board and installed his chief of staff, the vice president’s wife and the commerce secretary’s wife in their place. The new board got to work quickly: It ousted the center’s chairman — the private equity billionaire David Rubenstein, who had also given $111 million of his own money to the organization — and named Trump to that position. He is the first U.S. president to hold it.

Removing President Trump’s name from the facade of the Kennedy Center.  Pete Kiehart for The New York Times

Chaos. It was the era of DOGE in Washington, and Trump brought in Richard Grenell, his tough-talking former ambassador to Germany, to clean house. Grenell fired dozens of staffers and denounced the center’s programming in Trumpian terms. He also drastically pruned the size of the fund-raising office, taking it from 93 employees to 17. The Kennedy Center is a nonprofit and depends heavily on gifts to survive. Now donors didn’t want to give, and there were few workers to recruit their funds anyway. Performers began to cancel their shows. The Washington National Opera decided to cut ties with the center. Ticket sales and audiences plunged.

Rebranding. In place of the San Francisco Ballet and a touring production of “Hamilton,” the Kennedy Center turned to MAGA programming. It offered a discount so the American Conservative Union Foundation could convene a summit on Christian persecution. It let FIFA run the World Cup draw there for free. And Trump made himself the M.C. of the new Kennedy Center Honors. These went to Sylvester Stallone and the rock band Kiss, among others. During the night’s festivities, he jokingly called it “the Trump Kennedy Center,” to laughter. The next week, his board voted to put his name on the building. It was now the Donald J. Trump and the John F. Kennedy Memorial Center for the Performing Arts.

Legal warfare. Soon came the lawsuit: How could the president rename an organization that had been named by an act of Congress? In court, Trump’s lawyers argued it was simply recognition for his contributions to the health of the institution. But a judge in May said that wasn’t good enough to override a statute, and Trump’s name had to come down. For a president so focused on symbols, it was a bitter pill. Rather than show the building without Trump’s name on it, workers covered the facade.

Aboard Air Force One last month. Kenny Holston/The New York Times

A new claim. Having failed to leave his mark, Trump introduced a novel argument, saying that the building was unsafe and needed to close. In September, a heavy storm brought down a five-foot section of ceiling plaster in the foyer. Staffers circulated the video online and left the debris in the hall, then conducted an investigation. The administration said structural issues in the building were practically beyond repair. (The center has always undergone regular maintenance.)

The end? Last month, the board voted to close the facility where once there were daily performances. Today there are none. More than 2,000 people have lost their jobs. Trump now says the Kennedy Center may even be demolished. And even if it survives that threat, it could take years to rebuild the staff and its fund-raising muscle, much less its programming and audience. The future of this glittering showcase for American artistic achievement is very much in doubt.

A NIGHTMARE IN THE SKY

The New York Times

“I’m so screwed,” Peleg Itali, 16, recalled thinking. “I don’t deserve to die like this.” He was aboard a flight from Dubai to Tel Aviv yesterday when one pilot stabbed another. As the plane nose-dived, overhead bins swung open and parents clung to their children. The cockpit melee ended when passengers arrived to subdue the aggressor and minister to the injured pilot, who had wounds to his head and cuts on his fingers.

Emergency landing: Crew members and passengers managed to land the plane in Tabuk, Saudi Arabia. All the passengers arrived safely in Israel after a replacement aircraft retrieved them. Read the story.

Potential motives: It’s not clear what caused the struggle in the cockpit. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu of Israel said that one pilot had stabbed the other and “apparently tried to crash the plane with everyone on board,” nearly all of them Israelis. The Israeli authorities are investigating.

The pilots: Netanyahu identified the pilot who was stabbed as Capt. Smit Machchhar, an Indian citizen. Three Israeli officials said the other pilot, whom they believed to be the aggressor, was a citizen of Oman.
