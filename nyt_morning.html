Good morning. Today we want to show you some amazing images from yesterday’s total solar eclipse.

The eclipse as seen from Spain.  Christian Hartmann/Reuters

A celestial wonder

“Totality is a cosmic coincidence,” explains our science reporter Katrina Miller:

Our moon is about 400 times smaller than the sun, but it is also 400 times closer to Earth. This makes the two appear to be the same size from our point of view. The sun, moon and Earth are locked in a gravitational dance around one another, and when these three align, we bear witness to a solar eclipse.

That’s what happened yesterday in parts of Europe. Millions of people looked up to see the moon devour the sun, briefly turning the star that gives us life into a hazy glowing ring in the sky. Day suddenly turned to dark. The air cooled. Crowds cheered. Birds started shrieking. Then, just a couple of minutes later, everything returned to normal.

It was Europe’s first total solar eclipse in decades, a rare collective experience for almost everyone along the viewing path. True totality — the eerie, fleeting event where the sun and the moon perfectly align — could be seen in remote Greenland, in the cliff towns of Iceland and in parts of Spain.

At an eclipse watch party in Spain. Susana Vera/Reuters

As the moon appeared to slide across the surface of the sun, skywatchers who came prepared donned eclipse glasses and stared up.

Two ways of watching. Angelika Warmuth/Reuters; Susana Vera/Reuters

On Iceland’s west coast, the clouds parted just enough to allow a glimpse of the solar eclipse. First, the sun narrowed to a sliver, like a French tip on a celestial manicure. Then, it was suddenly twilight.

In the Spanish heartland, where large crowds gathered in normally empty towns, excited visitors chanted “Now, now, now!” as the moon took its first nibble. In Mallorca, the moment of totality arrived just before sundown, making it feel even more dramatic. An optical illusion causes the sun to look much larger as it nears the horizon, where the Earth’s atmosphere also shifts the color of the light.

In Mallorca. Francisco Ubilla/Reuters

In cities along the path of totality, the eclipse was one of the summer’s main attractions. My colleague Jason Karaian, who was in Mallorca, noticed a restaurant offering a burger with a side of eclipse-watching glasses for about $23.

The moment of totality. Borja Suarez/Reuters

Eclipses are special because they’re rare. The United States, for instance, won’t see a coast-to-coast eclipse until 2045. As Katrina put it:

It offers a primal sense of what it means to be human, through shared despair and delight under a celestial rebirth. We are all so consumed by our problems on the ground. Sometimes, we need a reason to look up.
