Strongest synoptic forcing with this feature still looks to migrate from northern IA/southern MN early this evening to the western Great Lakes overnight before shifting across southeast Ontario Thursday morning. Some pre-frontal forcing and narrow axis of low level moisture convergence accompanying a strong low level jet downstream of this feature should promote a narrow band of rain dropping across the region late tonight into Thursday AM. Through time Thursday morning, precip coverage should likely diminish as stronger synoptic support shifts off to the southeast Canada leaving this frontal boundary stalled across the area. Precip amounts (around tenth of an inch) late tonight into Thursday should not be of much consequence to water levels on area rivers. The stalled boundary is likely to become active once again Thursday night into Friday as another mid level wave drops southeast into the area through the downstream portion of the northern stream upper ridge across western CONUS. A ramping up of low level flow/moisture advection should allow for increase in showers and embedded storms accompanying northward retreating boundary Thursday night. Eventually, a cold frontal feature will shift back across the local area with likelihood of showers and embedded storms into Friday. Precip amounts in this round of precip should generally be one quarter of an inch or less and generally not a huge impact on area river trends. May need to watch for axis of heavier amounts of 0.50" or greater across Kankakee/St. Joseph basins from NE IL into SW Lower Michigan late Thursday night/early Friday when sfc boundary is more stationary and where low level jet may interact with this frontal zone for a time.

The low level baroclinic zone should get pushed south of the local area later Friday with primarily quiet conditions for at least first part of the weekend. Northward surging warm front for weekend combined with additional series of smaller scale embedded waves ejecting northeast in southwest upper flow will provide low chances of showers and storms for portions of the area Sunday.

By early next week, the main forecast focus shifts to the evolution of larger scale southern stream cut-off trough and its northeast ejection into central CONUS for Tuesday. This will support two warm days Sunday and Monday with highs back into the 75 to 88 degree range. The ejection of this trough should also draw some better mid level lapse rates northeast from the southern/central Plains once again and the anomalous moisture combined with these steeper lapse rates aloft should be more supportive of increased shower/storm chances early next week. Timing of mid/upper level forcing mechanisms remains an item of lower confidence so an extended period of high chance to likely PoPs had to be maintained early next week. Some strong/severe risk is possible for one of these periods early next week given above factors, but uncertainty in synoptic boundary placement and timing of these periods of stronger synoptic forcing still lead to low confidence in this regard. Confidence remains on the higher side in much above normal temps Sun-Tue likely trending back to near normal or slightly below normal toward the middle of next week.

02. nyt briefing

By Sam Sifton

Good morning. We’ll start today behind the moon, farther from Earth than anyone’s ever been.

At 6:44 p.m. Eastern, the crew of NASA’s moon mission Artemis II was cut off from the rest of humanity as the spaceship slipped behind the moon.

For about 40 minutes, no one on Earth could know exactly what the four astronauts were doing. During their interlude in silence, they ventured farther from our planet than anyone who has ever lived. When the spacecraft emerged on the other side, the crew watched as a thin crescent of sunlit Earth rose above the lunar surface.

Since launching to space on Wednesday, the astronauts of Artemis II have relayed observations back to scientists on Earth. They have tangled with the vehicle’s toilet and taken dazzling photos of space. Before their loop around the moon, they also radioed Houston with a request. They asked to name a previously undesignated crater in memory of the wife of Reid Wiseman, the mission’s commander. She died of cancer in 2020.

“It’s a bright spot on the moon, and we would like to call it Carroll,” said Jeremy Hansen, the mission specialist, his voice breaking up. The astronauts embraced and wiped their eyes. For a moment, mission control went silent.

WAR IN IRAN

One of the most difficult things to understand about a faraway conflict is its scale — its size relative to our own lived experience. The graphic designer Massimo Vignelli had a line about that. He said that dimensions are physical, but scale is mental. I always took that to mean that scale exists in the imagination, a frame of mind that tells us more than a list of measurements or a roster of numbers.

Take the war in the Middle East. My colleague Martín González Gómez, a graphics editor, wanted to understand its scale. To show it, he took maps of the conflict and laid them over maps of other parts of the world.

The total land area of the countries shooting at each other would cover much of the continental United States. In the overlay, you can see that strikes have happened over what amounts to the distance between Florida and Oregon.

Russia invaded Ukraine four years ago, and each side has fought bitterly over narrow tracts of land. Iran is significantly larger than Ukraine and has more than double its population.

Martín laid a map of the Strait of Hormuz, through which a fifth of the world’s supply of oil used to run, over a map of New York City. The narrow shipping channels that Iran has under its control are each about the width of a Manhattan neighborhood.

These maps will help give you a sense of perspective.

Chicken Manchurian is South Asia’s rejoinder to food court Chinese chicken in America: a sticky, spicy-sweet Desi Chinese stir-fry dependent on the flavors of caramelized ketchup, chile and garlic.
