Tranquil weather continues today with dry conditions and low humidity. High pressure current centered over the Upper Midwest will slowly build across the Great Lakes in the coming days. Dry conditions are expected to continue through Saturday with this high pressure system eventually overhead. High temperatures today will be a bit warmer than yesterday but still only topping out in the upper 70s! Today and tonight will be great opportunities to open up windows, with temperatures dropping into the 50s once again overnight. Lows in the 50s, while unusual from the heat and humidity we have been experiencing recently, are not that uncommon in July. We typically average about 5 nights with lows below 60 degrees in July.

Temperatures trend much warmer into the weekend. Moisture increases as WAA ramps up, which means humidity starts increasing too. Hot and humid conditions return by Sunday and Monday with highs in the upper 80s to low 90s with dewpoints in the low to mid 70s. Increased evapotranspiration from crops may also aid in locally higher dewpoints. Our forecast area will be split in between a trough to our east and a large upper level ridge to our west. Pattern recognition tells me this is a favorable set up for a potentially active pattern into early next week with northwest flow aloft. A frontal system brings the next chances for rain/storms late Sunday into Monday, although confidence is uncertain on exact timing. Models have been trending drier for Sunday with precipitation chances that currently sit around 20%; Monday`s chances are higher at 20-60%. Any storms that develop may be able to ride the edge of the upper level ridge through our area. If we can get sufficent instability and moisture, severe weather is not out of the question for Sunday and especially Monday. Confidence is very low given the high spread in deterministic and ensemble model guidance.

02. nyt briefing

By Sam Sifton

Good morning. As the war between the United States and Iran continues, I want to look at how The Times uses photographs and video to better understand what’s happening on the ground.

Here are two sentences that jumped out at me yesterday from our live coverage of the war:

The Iranian military said in statements carried by the country’s state news media early Wednesday that it had targeted U.S. military facilities in Kuwait, Jordan and Bahrain. There were no immediate reports of damage or casualties from the U.S. military or the authorities in any of the three countries.

What struck me was how familiar that sounded. We heard this week about the deaths of four U.S. service members, soldiers assigned to missile defense work. But we haven’t heard very much at all about the extent of damage Iranian strikes have caused to American military bases in the region.

And it’s hard to find. The Pentagon hasn’t held a major news briefing since early May. There’s not a lot of high-resolution satellite imagery. The Trump administration asked major U.S. satellite providers to withhold visual images of the region.

Our visual investigations team, however, was able to assess the damage to two U.S. sites in Jordan by comparing images from disparate sources. Iran, for instance, often releases satellite imagery of strikes as part of its propaganda campaign. The Times journalists corroborated those images using videos captured from inside and outside the sites, along with photography and lower-resolution satellite imagery they were able to gather.

Here are some key findings from their investigation — the Pentagon, by the way, declined to comment:

Three of the soldiers killed last week were stationed at Muwaffaq Salti Air Base in Jordan.

After the attacks there on Friday night, a video appeared on social media showing a projectile exploding near several buildings. The original source of the footage is unknown, but The Times was able to verify it, and reports that it was likely filmed by personnel on the base.

Iran later released several satellite images that showed significant damage to the station, and The Times assessed it using a range of visual evidence, including satellite imagery from the European Space Agency. At least 20 structures appeared completely or partially destroyed.

Iran also struck Tower 22, a U.S. military outpost on Jordan’s border with Syria. The destruction there first appeared in low-resolution European satellite imagery that my colleagues corroborated, and then in a higher-resolution satellite image published by Iranian state media showing that one of the base’s buildings had been badly damaged. The Times corroborated that, too.

A video that was shared on Telegram, and which was verified by The Times, also showed a building reduced to rubble at Tower 22. It is unclear if the strike caused casualties.

Cartographic evidence

You can learn a lot looking at those kinds of pictures. At maps, too.

Check out the ones my colleagues made to show how the threat of a Houthi blockade in the Red Sea could again upend global oil markets by compromising a crucial fallback for the Strait of Hormuz.

More on the war: Oil prices surged after the Houthis said they had attacked two Saudi tankers in the Red Sea. Read the latest news.

A PERSONAL RECKONING

One more war story. Back in 2010, Thomas Gibbons-Neff was in the Marine Corps, the 22-year-old leader of a scout sniper team in one of the largest operations of the war against the Taliban: the battle of Marja. He’s spent the past decade as a reporter covering wars and gun culture in the United States, first for The Washington Post and, since 2017, here at The Times. Now he is going back to school to become an English teacher.

As he prepared to leave The Times, Gibbons-Neff returned to Afghanistan to speak with the people he fought against, in search of answers and maybe closure. “I’ve had a hand in killing around six people,” he writes. “Maybe seven. I don’t know if one man bled out on the side of the road or was taken to a doctor. I think about him often.”

His is a searing, difficult, somehow beautiful story. I urge you to read it closely, all the way through.

THE LATEST NEWS

  • The Trump administration signed a nuclear agreement with Saudi Arabia that could allow the kingdom to enrich its own uranium.
  • Under the terms of the deal, Saudi Arabia does not have to allow international inspectors into its facilities — a requirement that applies to more than 140 countries.
  • The deal is a coup for Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, who has pushed for years to obtain American nuclear technology.
  • Israel is concerned that the deal will encourage other countries to develop nuclear programs — and experts say that could lead to an arms race in the Middle East.
  • The House adopted Republicans’ $95 billion budget plan to fund the war in Iran and impose new voting restrictions demanded by President Trump. It could face an uphill battle in the Senate.
  • The chamber also approved a $1.15 trillion military spending bill over the opposition of Democrats, who said backing it was akin to embracing the war in Iran. Without significant revisions, it lacks votes to pass in the Senate.
  • Also passed in the House: a bill to limit stock trading by members of Congress.
  • The Trump administration canceled grants to teen pregnancy programs, saying they encouraged premarital sex. The new proposal? Teach abstinence only.
  • Tens of thousands of immigrants have received letters from the federal government demanding that they pay up to $1.8 million in fines or leave the country.
  • The gallery at the Louvre Museum in Paris where thieves took crown jewels worth $100 million reopened to the public. The only difference: no gems.
  • In the Democratic Republic of Congo, Ebola seems to be spreading in the cramped camps that house more than a million displaced people.
  • European Union regulators fined Google $1 billion for using its position as the world’s largest search engine to unfairly promote its own services.
  • A Florida pastor sued OpenAI, claiming that advice from ChatGPT led him to delay treatment for a life-threatening embolism. It appears to be the first case arguing that a chatbot harmed someone seeking medical advice.
  • Scientists in Vermont have identified a contagious form of skin cancer in catfish. They recommend not eating the fish.
