02. nyt briefing

Good morning. We’ll get to the news below. But first, we want to answer your questions.

It’s one of the great perks of working at The New York Times: If you have a question, someone in the newsroom probably knows the answer. Readers, unfortunately, can’t just walk up to our colleagues’ desks and ask. That’s where we come in.

Today, we have another collection of questions from Morning readers, with answers from Times journalists. If you have a question for one of our reporters, ask it here. We may feature it in the next edition.

The Treasury announced a new gold coin with President Trump’s face on it. I thought there was a law against pictures of living people on currency. What’s the truth around that?— Howard Stone, Boynton Beach, Florida

Alan Rappeport, who covers economic policy in Washington, answers:

The controversy about the legality of Trump’s face appearing on the gold coin rests on the question of whether or not a coin counts as currency. In 1866, Congress passed a law stating that “only the portrait of a deceased individual may appear on United States currency and securities.” The Trump administration is leaning into the argument that a coin is different from paper currency or a security. And it is not even clear who would have standing to take the administration to court to kill the commemorative coin (which, for the record, contains no actual gold).

When GLP-1s first hit the market, there was speculation that they could be used to effectively treat addiction, but I haven’t heard anything recently. Are there any clinical trials underway?— Lucinda Duzan, Glen Rock, Pennsylvania

Dani Blum, a health reporter who covers weight-loss drugs, answers:

There’s been a lot of momentum around GLP-1s for substance use disorders lately, particularly when it comes to alcohol. The Department of Veterans Affairs just launched a trial testing semaglutide, the active substance in Ozempic and Wegovy, for people with alcohol use disorder — I’ll be closely watching those results. And a few study results have trickled out over the last few years suggesting that GLP-1s could curb cravings for alcohol. Still, scientists urge caution: We’re still in the early stages of studying this.

What is going on with the Department of Education currently?— Kelley Ellis, Denver

Michael C. Bender, who covers the department, answers:

The Education Department still exists, it’s just harder to find. Trump wants to completely eliminate the agency, but that requires an act of Congress, where lawmakers have shown little interest in such a change. His workaround has been to scatter the Education Department’s main functions across six other executive agencies.

Education Secretary Linda McMahon remains in her job, along with her top lieutenants, but she operates now almost like a contractor, hiring the Health and Human Services Department, the Treasury and others to effectively employ Education Department staff members and carry out the department’s statutory duties.

Is there a reason the United States does not have a single Primary Day? It seems to lend a great deal of influence to a handful of states, which in turn seems less democratic.— Jacqueline Kitka, Haverhill, Massachusetts

Katie Glueck, a national politics reporter, answers:

You’re right that a handful of states have long played an outsize role in determining presidential nominees. The order of the early-voting states has at times been a source of debate and drama, especially for the Democrats. They have wrestled for years with how to reflect the party’s, and the country’s, diversity in their lineup.

As for why there’s no single national Primary Day? I turned to Elaine Kamarck, who wrote a book on the presidential primary system, “Primary Politics,” and is a member of the committee that has been working on the Democrats’ next primary lineup. In short, she said, it’s a constitutional issue.

“This started out, and still remains constitutionally, in the purview of the states,” she told me. “In order to get all the states to be on one page, you’d have to have an act of Congress, and Congress has been very reluctant to ever interfere in that.”

IN ONE CHART

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has repeatedly blocked promotions for high-ranking women in the military. This year, a Times analysis found, the percentage of such nominations for women is the lowest in at least 25 years.

Hegseth has not said why he blocked the promotions, but he has argued that the Pentagon’s past diversity efforts degraded the military. “For too long, we’ve promoted too many uniform leaders for the wrong reasons,” he told military leaders last September. “Based on their race, based on gender quotas, based on historic so-called firsts.”
