.DISCUSSION... Issued at 200 AM EDT Sun Aug 16 2026

Latest water vapor imagery shows one more potent MCV ejecting out of Iowa early this morning. Regional radar mosaic currently shows a bowing segment but mesoanalysis indicates very limited shear and therefore unlikely to pose a severe risk. Once again our current environment is very stable but another round of strengthening nocturnal LLJ will advect higher theta-e air back into the area early this morning. This should support at least some elevated convection roughly 08-15Z this morning. Convection will likely tend to outpace better surface-based instability but there`s at least some low chances that a slightly later passage could allow some diurnal contribution in the 14-17Z timeframe that would yield a gusty wind threat for our far E/NE counties. This is suggested by some runs of the HRRR but still think it will be difficult to get sufficient instability that far NE that early to support severe storms. As for the hydro threat...best overall rain chances look to be in our northern CWA and storms will likely be much more progressive and scattered than in recent days due to the current synoptic setup. The threat of true "heavy" rain is very low but susceptibility to even moderate rain in our southern CWA is still high. Given this and some lingering uncertainty on where exactly storms track later this morning plan to just let the existing flood watch ride for now.

Attention this afternoon and evening then turns to very broad/ diffuse cold frontal passage. Ample low level moisture and some steepening midlevel lapse rates generate over 2000 j/kg of SBCAPE in our southern zones this afternoon and 25-30kts of deep-layer shear would suggest a threat for organized convection. The problem is the forcing is just very weak. We actually have some shortwave ridging in the wake of this morning MCV that persists well into the evening and low level convergence is virtually nil. Hi-res CAM`s subsequently show very isolated convection this afternoon and evening. Can`t entirely rule out a stray strong storm in our south given the instability and shear parameters but risk appears very low at this point.

Finally we return to quiet weather for Monday and Tuesday. The next chance of storms arrives Tue night. A deep upper low is forecast to cross northern Ontario with modest height falls extending into the Great Lakes region. Associated fast midlevel flow yields some impressive shear but instability will be in doubt for our area. General WNW flow ahead of the front limits theta-e recovery and nighttime arrival limits the diurnal contribution. An earlier arrival could yield a threat for stronger storms in our far NW Tue evening but chances for that currently appear low. This front will be slow moving and rain chances extend through the day Wed but lackluster midlevel lapse rates and low level moisture return suggest a low risk for both severe weather and heavy rain given the current timing.

Forecast confidence isn`t high for the end of the week given some fairly large model differences but there is a general lean toward cooler, drier, and more tranquil weather.

Good morning. More people are using A.I. to handle tasks on their behalf. Are we ready for a world in which machines are mostly talking to one another?

By Callie Holtermann

I’m a flesh-and-blood reporter on the Style desk, and I wrote this newsletter without A.I.

If you haven’t applied for a job recently, you might be surprised by the new common procedure. At one end, job seekers use A.I. chatbots to churn out résumés and cover letters. At the other, employers use A.I. to sift through all of those applications. People on each side of this equation say they need these tools to keep up with the other. But the result feels hollow, impersonal, frustrating. Are people actually getting to know each other better?

This sort of thing is happening everywhere. Consider a high school student who asks an A.I. chatbot to write an essay that she submits to a teacher, who then uses A.I. to grade it. Or a person who asks a chatbot to write a witty opening line to a romantic prospect, who then uses a chatbot to generate a flirty response. Or a person who sends his A.I. agent to go argue with a company’s A.I. customer service system.

“We’ve officially reached the point where customer support is just two LLMs locked in a recursive loop of ‘I understand your concern,’ while the actual humans on both ends just watch the credits roll,” one Reddit user wrote recently.

Many of us have spent the past couple of years getting used to the strangeness of humans adopting chatbots as conversational partners. But now, as more of us complete everyday tasks with A.I., all of those directives are bumping up against one another. I’ve started calling these situations “bot loops” — the little whirlpools that spin up whenever humans on each side of an interaction outsource their roles to A.I.

My bot, my self

Some people are hopeful about the potential for these bot-on-bot interactions to streamline our work and personal lives. It’s not crazy to think that an A.I. voice agent could someday call the Department of Motor Vehicles for me, and that another A.I. agent could answer the phone. If it saves me a trip to my local D.M.V. in Brooklyn, where dreams go to die, I’m certainly interested.

Whitney Wolfe Herd, the chief executive of the dating app Bumble, has suggested that our A.I. personas will eventually go on dates with each other and report back to us with the results. (When one of my colleagues tried this, it did not go especially well. Better luck next time, Eli!)

Still, the experts I talked to for my new story about bot loops in The Times Magazine told me there’s a lot to worry about. A.I. systems get things wrong; string a bunch together and they could amplify one another’s errors. And there’s fascinating research emerging about so-called A.I. self-preference bias — the tendency for these systems to favor their own work. In other words, an A.I. recruiter will probably rate a résumé generated by ChatGPT more highly than one written by a human.

But the thing that unsettled me most while reporting was the way that bot loops strain human relationships. The journalist Evan Ratliff thinks it’s hard to blame job applicants for using A.I. when companies don’t even bother to send real interviewers. “Once it’s a little bit debased, you do eliminate the incentive for people to show up as their authentic selves,” he said.

It can be nerve-racking to show up if you don’t know whom (or what) you’ll be dealing with. Ratliff has spent a lot of time experimenting with A.I. voice agents for his podcast, “Shell Game.” When I first got on the phone with him, I found myself scrutinizing his small talk to determine whether I was speaking with him or his bot. A few minutes later, we were both laughing. It turned out he had been wondering the same thing about me.
