By Sam Sifton

Good morning. The left scored another upset last night: A democratic socialist won the Democratic Senate primary in Florida. And President Trump delayed his tariffs on Canada.

There’s more below. First, let’s go to Hawaii.

Hurricane Lala tore past the Big Island of Hawaii last weekend, dropped three feet of rain and left a path of ruins that residents will be working for months to fix.

Times journalists yesterday reported on two places where that work’s afoot. One story’s a feel-good portrait of the Hawaiian principles of aloha, which means love and compassion, and ohana, which means family and community. The other might break your heart.

At the gas station

Orlando Mayorquín, a reporter in our Los Angeles bureau, traveled to a small mountain town on the southern end of the island to write about the parking lot of the Ocean View Market gas station, where locals this week set up a spontaneous disaster relief center.

What he found was remarkable. The gas station was closed. Tim Reed, an Ocean View resident who runs a low-power radio station that usually plays Hawaiian music and classic rock, had connected his equipment to a satellite to broadcast information about the hub — including his offer to let residents connect to the internet via Starlink and make calls to the outside world. “We’re the emergency communication for Ocean View,” he told Orlando.

Katie Tyner, another resident, had become “the gas lady.” Orlando found her standing next to her S.U.V., writing down names and numbers on a clipboard. “Her operation was simple and in high demand in a community where many do not own cars,” Orlando reported: “Hand her cash and a fuel can and she would return with gas from an operational station half an hour up the road.”

There was Pamela Ako, a pastor, handing out water bottles and accepting food and supplies for people who might need them: batteries, rice flour, instant Korean noodles, rope. “It’s people helping people,” she told Orlando. “This is when we come together as a family. You talk about aloha; you talk about ohana — this is what it’s all about.”

Kahi Young rolled up then. He had persuaded a local helicopter tour company to bring supplies to another small town, about 25 miles away. The aircraft would arrive soon in a nearby park, and Young needed help loading hundreds of pounds of food and gear. Ako sent him to Reed, who put him on air.

Soon volunteers were filling the helicopter with boxes of toilet paper and baby wipes, along with lots of ready-to-eat food.

Read more about the scene in Ocean View here.

Cemetery road

Meghan Miner Murray, a journalist in Hawaii who contributes to The Times, reported from the hamlet of Naalehu, roughly 12 miles east of Ocean View. Flooding from the storm inundated a small cemetery there, pulling both gravestones and coffins from the earth and leaving behind a sea of mud and wreckage.

The scene was chilling. What Meghan saw: “A body stripped of clothes lay crushed beneath a boulder. About a half dozen caskets were visible, most empty and torn apart. The smell of the unearthed dead suffused parts of the debris field.” Flotsam was everywhere. She saw refrigerators in the mud, clothes, a sodden mattress, a hammer.

Families searched the site, looking for the graves of their loved ones. Maka Gallinger came with eight of her relatives. “We’re going to find my mom,” she told Meghan. “We got to know. We’ve got to take care of our family bones.”

All was not horror, though. Gallinger’s mother’s grave, at least, was untouched. “Here she is,” Gallinger said when she found it. “All right, Mom. We’re just checking.”

    RECIPE OF THE DAY

    I love Kevin Pang’s recipe for sesame soy chicken with ginger-scallion noodles, in part because you don’t need to make it with chicken, nor with noodles. As long as you have the ginger-scallion sauce as a keystone, you’ll be eating well. (Last night’s attempt had me serving it over rice and a fried egg, with dried seaweed flakes and a flash of soy sauce.) Man, it’s good with the chicken and noodles, though.
