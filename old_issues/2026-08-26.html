.DISCUSSION... Issued at 315 AM EDT Wed Aug 26 2026

Convective/severe weather potential today will continue to be the primary forecast concern for the short term period.

Clouds are expected to increase from west to east this morning as a zone of low level warm/moist advection across Wisconsin/northern Illinois builds eastward aligning with a zone of stronger 800 mb positive theta-e advection. Convection early this morning is limited to portions of southwest Wisconsin in closer proximity to cold frontal boundary and where some DPVA is ongoing in advance of an upper level low centered across the arrowhead of Minnesota. Through the morning, expecting some increase in low level forcing with a pre-frontal trough type feature shifting across the western Great Lakes. Some isolated- scattered convection is possible later this morning/early afternoon along this pre-frontal feature, particularly across NW Indiana into southwest Lower Michigan. Instability through early afternoon should be quite limited and not expecting any threat of severe weather through early afternoon if convection does develop.

The primary mid/upper level trough and stronger mid/upper level forcing should be across central/northern Lower MI today, but southern Lower Michigan/northern Indiana will be located on southern extent of mid/upper level height falls accompanying this trough. Some increase in deep layer shear is expected this morning as modest increases in 500 mb flow to 30-40 knots is expected resulting in increasing deep layer shear to around 30 knots. Some moderate MLCAPE on the order of 2000-2500 J/kg is possible this afternoon, but likely oriented in narrow axis just ahead of the cold front as mid level lapse rates across the area remain on the marginal side. Some question persists regarding the extent of destabilization with a possibility that some pre-frontal cloudiness/convection could prevent instability magnitudes from reaching this potential. There is also some question as initiation time and coverage of convection as low level convergence fields may be a bit more limited across the local area as stronger mid/upper level forcing remains just north of the local area. Combination of moderate instability potential near frontal zone and sufficient deep layer shear could support isolated-scattered severe thunderstorm risk this afternoon into early evening. Damaging wind gusts to 60 mph appear to be the primary threat given steep 0-3km lapse rates, but some isolated large hail is possible especially closer to convective initiation time. However, limited mid level lapse rates may limit the hail potential. Given instability/shear combination some supercell structure/isolated tornado potential is possible, although guidance has trended to a bit more veered nature to near sfc flow over past several runs which could limit low level helicity from earlier indications. The primary severe risk still looks to be largely in the 19Z-02Z timeframe, and particularly through 00Z.

Loss of connection to stronger sfc based instability should diminish intensity of frontal convection after 00Z or 01Z this evening. Some patchy fog development is possible late tonight into early Thursday morning behind the sfc front with weak low level height gradient and residual near sfc moisture likely remaining in place. Another weak mid level trough will dive southeast in wake of today`s upper trough for Thursday keeping the local area in broad cyclonic flow. Some surface based instability should be present in the afternoon and some possibility of isolated showers could materialize. Have maintained silent 10-14 PoPs this cycle given rather nebulous mid level forcing, but it is possible some low PoPs will be needed Thursday PM in later cycles.

Friday should bring dry conditions as mid level ridging across central CONUS builds eastward. Positioning of this ridge upstream of the area should keep a modifying low level thermal trough in place with another day of seasonable temperatures on Friday. The weekend still looks to feature warmer conditions as favored storm track remains across northern tier of CONUS resulting in dampening of the upstream ridge and eventual return of low level warm air/moisture advection. This pattern evolution should support eventual advection of some warm mid level temperatures across the southern Great Lakes and Ohio Valley. This leads to low confidence in instability profiles for the weekend into early next week. Some potential will exist for sfc frontal boundary to approach the region in this pattern, but confidence in depth of frontal lift is in question given background synoptic pattern. Thus, low chance/slight chance PoPs will be maintained for much of the days 4-7 period along with transition to above normal temps.

02. nyt briefing

By Sam Sifton

Good morning. Senator Darline Graham, who filled the seat of her brother, Lindsey Graham, after his death, won the Republican Senate nomination in South Carolina. President Trump had backed her.

And there’s more below, including our obituary of Dolly Parton and an accounting of 15 of her best songs.

I’m going to start, though, with the president.

President Trump’s usual playbook — make demands, threaten Armageddon, force capitulation — is not working.

Canada said yesterday that it would impose tariffs as high as 50 percent on hundreds of American products, after the United States imposed its own levies on Canadian goods. The prime minister walked away from trade talks last week, and The Times chronicled how Canadians had his back. “We’re standing our ground not to be bullied on the world stage and pushed around,” a shop owner in Manitoba told our reporter.

In Washington, congressional Republicans who have usually deferred to Trump blasted him for raising imports of ground beef. It may bring the price of a burger down before the midterm elections. But legislators warn it would be at the expense of American ranchers, and that’s intolerable. “Americans want U.S. beef on the table — not foreign imports,” Senator John Barrasso of Wyoming said in a statement.

Peter Baker, who covers the Trump presidency, continues the list of defiance:

European leaders have refused to join his war with Iran. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu of Israel flatly rejected his Gaza plan. Gulf Arab states forced him to abandon plans to charge a shipping toll in the Strait of Hormuz.

Universities and law firms are rebuffing his directives rather than signing on the bottom line. Bond markets are rebelling against his fiscal policies. Even the ABC network, whose $16 million payment to settle a Trump lawsuit was seen as an early example of yielding to the president, has discovered that it did not buy peace and is now suing his administration for threatening its broadcast licenses.

None of which means Trump should be underestimated, Peter warns. His polls may be low, and the influence of his political endorsements may have slipped, but Trump’s still powerful. The Supreme Court’s generally kind to him. He was able to get Todd Blanche confirmed as attorney general. Darline Graham is the Republican nominee for Senate in South Carolina. He posts on social media, and markets move.

Still, the resistance is real. “Last spring, I warned that America is trying to break us so they can own us, and I promised that ‘That will never, ever happen,’” Canada’s prime minister, Mark Carney, said on Saturday, after abandoning the trade talks. “We are keeping that promise.”

But wait a minute. Didn’t the Supreme Court say Trump’s tariffs were illegal anyway? Alisha Anderko, a Morning reader from Pittsburgh, wrote in with a smart question: “So how can he enact a new 50 percent tariff on certain Canadian goods?”

Ana Swanson, who covers trade, was game to answer:

The Supreme Court did overturn Trump’s use of one law to impose global tariffs, an international emergency law. But other laws give him the power to issue tariffs, including Section 338, the provision he used for the Canada tariffs. It’s nearly 100 years old, and it lets the president impose levies in response to discriminatory trade practices. But it’s very likely to face court challenges in the coming weeks, and it’s unclear whether it will survive.

DOLLY PARTON, 1946-2026

Dolly Parton’s life was the stuff of fable, from her hardscrabble beginnings in Appalachia — her family was so poor that her father paid the doctor who attended her birth with a sack of cornmeal — to her glittering stardom. She was the top-ranking female singer on country radio from 1967 to 1993, with a total of 25 No. 1 country singles, and she earned entry into both the country music and the rock ’n’ roll halls of fame.

Parton cannily exploited rural stereotypes and kitsch to fashion a down-home chic entirely her own. “I’ve created this and played it up — the makeup, the whole persona,” she told The Times in 2002. “But this isn’t all I am,” she added. “It’s not even most of what I am. Hopefully people can see beneath the hair to know there’s a brain, beneath the boobs to know there’s a heart, and behind all the other stuff to know there’s some talent.”

She died at 80 after a cancer diagnosis. Read her obituary.

    With climate change intensifying our seasons, and the government showing little interest in addressing its causes, are there any technologies emerging that could help manage the extreme temperatures and weather changes? — Lorraine Yund, Pittsburgh

    Some interventions to modify the weather remain controversial, such as blocking some sunlight from reaching the earth. But extreme conditions are inspiring new innovations. I recently wrote about how drought-stricken regions hire companies to seed clouds, a practice that can squeeze more rain or snow out of storms by injecting clouds with particles or electric charges. Researchers are exploring similar approaches to weaken hurricanes and reduce the size of hail. Devices can pull potable water out of the air. And low-tech solutions like strategic tree planting and light-colored pavement are helping cities to reduce heat. — Scott Dance, a climate reporter
