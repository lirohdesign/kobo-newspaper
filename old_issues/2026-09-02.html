The primary forcing mechanism could be a departing 500-mb jet streak, but this focuses primary across central MI and into Ontario. Meanwhile, a second group of CAMs suggests renewed, isoalted activity along the IL-IN border in the 4p-6p ET window. This might be forced by a lingering outflow/MCV from the aformentioned upstream activity. Will pass this on to the incoming shift for further consideration.

Otherwise, very hot and humid once again today with highs in the low- 90s, dew points well into the 70s resulting in heat indicies of 100- 105 (as seen yesterday).

On Thursday, the 500mb ridge folds somewhat in response to a disturbance wrapping up over interior Ontario. The result locally is an increasing chance for showers and thunderstorms as the day progresses. Timing is uncertain, with some guidance favoring the overnight period instead of the late-afternoon. Our hot and humid environment will be supportive of isolated severe thunderstorms, but this risk is highly conditional on how any upstream activity evolves. These storms also decrease forecast confidence in the temperature forecast for Thursday. Due to anticipated cloud debris, we`ll hold off on any extension of the heat advisory.

With the upper-level ridge somewhat displaced, we could remain within an active period into at least the start of the Labor Day weekend with showers and storms around both Friday and Saturday. Beyond this, there is some indication the ridge may wash back north early next week. High temperatures Monday and Tuesday have a comically large spread (10+ degrees Tuesday) among the guidance, with the in-house blend on the cool-side. With that much spread, precipitation chances are likewise unclear. Given the trough working its way across the Cascades and through Montana, I would favor the ridge axis to revisit Indiana early in the week. No signs of fall quite yet.

02. nyt briefing

By Sam Sifton

Good morning. The United States carried out a new wave of attacks against Iran, and officials there said civilians had been killed at a wedding.

There’s more news below. I’m going to start, though, with the bond market.

Global bond yields are rising sharply. Um, what does that mean? We’re talking bonds today? Isn’t this a general-interest newsletter?

Investors are anxious about inflation and the amount of money governments have borrowed. So the institutions and funds that buy bonds — basically, loans you give the government — are demanding a higher return, a higher yield, to compensate them for that risk. Yesterday, the higher yields lifted borrowing costs in some of the world’s biggest economies to the highest levels in decades.

You’re going to feel that in the coming months. The bond market underpins the economy. In some ways, it is the economy. Your mortgage rate is tied to the yield on government bonds. So’s your car loan, your retirement plan, the new factory a company wants to build. “There is almost nothing financial they do not touch,” Ezra Klein said about Treasury bonds a few days ago on his Times podcast.

Governments get jittery when bond yields rise, as we’ve seen in the Trump administration. It suggests lenders are becoming more reluctant to lend the government money. Scott Bessent, the Treasury secretary, has tried to reassure investors that the nation’s fiscal health means bond holders will still make money from those investments. They don’t always believe.

A moneymaker

But wait. I’d have thought getting a better return on a bond was the whole purpose of investment. Wouldn’t a higher yield be good?

Ben Casselman, the chief economics correspondent for The Times, laughed when I asked him that yesterday. “I get it,” he said. “It’s counterintuitive.” Then he escorted me to a conference room, sat me down like a grade schooler and started to talk. I took notes:

When a government needs to raise money that it hasn’t raised in taxes, it sells bonds. At the most basic level: I give the government $1,000 for a fixed period of time, and in return they give me a fixed rate of interest. They pay that interest regularly over the course of the period — say, 10 years — and at its end give me my $1,000 back.

What’s cool is, I can sell my bond to you, and you get the interest payments for however much time is left on the bond. The market for that is enormous — over a trillion dollars in Treasury bonds are exchanged every day. It’s the most risk-free financial instrument that exists. And every loan that gets made in the economy is benchmarked off its yield.

As for why those yields are rising, he said, the simplest explanation is: inflation. Take Ben’s example. If you think inflation during that 10-year period is going to be at 4 percent instead of 2 percent, then you’re going to want a higher yield, because at the end of 10 years your $1,000 is going to be worth less than when you started — and the whole point was to make money on the deal.

So here’s the cycle, he said. The government sells bonds to pay its bills. It makes debt payments on them — more than it spends on the military, currently — and then issues more bonds to make those payments. Rinse and repeat. To break this dependence, we either reduce deficits or expand the economy. And watch out for inflation along the way! No wonder folks are nervous.

Your money

My colleagues Ron Lieber and Tara Siegel Bernard write about personal finance. They took on the question of what havoc in the bond market means for those of us sitting at home, worried about our investment and retirement portfolios. There’s a reason people own bonds, they write: “Bonds have had lower returns than stocks over time, but they also lack the volatility of stocks.”

How do we know what bonds we have and what’s happening with them now? Look at the paperwork.

“Let’s say you put money away for retirement in a target-date fund, an investment vehicle that is named for the year you hope to stop working,” they write. “Those funds have a mix of stock, bond and sometimes other funds.” You can look online and see how they’re doing. If you’re in the Vanguard Target Retirement 2040 Fund, for instance, you’re still up for the year. But if a financial adviser has purchased bonds for you, Ron and Tara counsel, then “ask for an accounting and an explanation of the strategy.”

Yikes. That sounds stressful. I’m glad my wife does that job.

    What is the difference between the agenda of the Democratic Socialists and the agenda of the Democratic Party? | Marilyn McIntyre | Huntley, Illinois

    The Democratic Socialists of America (D.S.A.) is an activist organization, not a political party, so its members typically run as Democrats. This has prompted confusion about the two groups. Many mainstream Democrats and D.S.A. members want to move in the same economic direction — raising taxes on wealthy Americans, increasing the minimum wage and expanding health care access. But while most Democrats consider themselves capitalists, D.S.A. members envision ultimately accomplishing those goals by establishing public ownership of the largest corporations. The D.S.A. agenda is also further to the left than most Democrats on social and foreign policy issues. It includes policies like ending all aid to Israel, closing overseas military bases and fully abolishing the police and prison systems. — Lisa Lerer, national politics correspondent
