02. nyt briefing

By Sam Sifton

Good morning from London, which enjoyed its rainiest day in months yesterday: proper pleasure and pain.

There’s more news below — including the outcome of a match at the U.S. Open that ended, like, four hours ago. But today I want to start in Germany.

You may have missed it. I almost did, amid the pleasures of a long weekend: The anti-immigrant Alternative for Germany party won big in a small state election on Sunday and could become the first far-right party to govern a German state since the end of World War II.

How big a deal is that? Our Berlin bureau chief, Jim Tankersley, offers one way of looking at it: The AfD’s win puts the mainstream conservative who is the German chancellor, Friedrich Merz, into a fight for political survival.

Merz is supposed to hold power until 2029, and his allies aren’t trying to get rid of him. But pressure is rising on his right flank, and more regional elections this month may show that voters see him “the wrong man for the job at this time,” as a major German newsmagazine put it. The AfD’s success, however isolated, is clearly a problem for Germany’s leadership.

It is also a problem for Germany, my colleague Katrin Bennhold wrote yesterday. I appreciated the wide view she took:

When I was growing up in Germany, the conventional wisdom was that the far right would never again gain a meaningful foothold in our politics. Our Nazi past, the argument went, had immunized voters against the appeal of such parties. And our post-Holocaust constitution had powerful tools to help prevent it. The rise of the anti-immigrant, far-right AfD over the past decade has made clear the first argument was wrong. The second? We’ll see.

Katrin crunched some numbers. On Sunday, the AfD didn’t just win more than twice as many votes as its nearest rival. It also won with the biggest regional voter turnout that Germany has seen since reunification in 1990.

In a lot of places, those facts would add up to a mandate to govern. But not in Germany, which has for nearly eight decades used a kind of firewall to prevent the rise of parties that the political mainstream deems extreme. It doesn’t want another authoritarian, another Hitler, to gain power. But now it faces a question. Here’s how Katrin put it:

Are these efforts to sideline the AfD in the name of democracy actually undermining democracy?

AfD’s rise

It’s an interesting question, perhaps a scary one. The AfD came into being in 2013, a year when Angela Merkel, the center-right chancellor at the time, went back into coalition with her party’s traditional opponents on the center left. That meant, basically, that whatever party a German voted for would result in the same government. The AfD, with its angry, nationalist, populist stance, represented a protest vote — functionally meaningless.

No longer. Its ranks swelled in number and passion. Now it’s the most popular party in the country.

In other words, the firewall could be falling. And that, Katrin writes, suggests that the AfD’s victory on Sunday could mark a new phase in German politics, one “in which mainstream parties accept that the way to defeat the AfD is to offer voters a platform they actually want to vote for.”

    RECIPE OF THE DAY

    I had an outrageously good dinner at Ballymaloe in Cork, Ireland, this past weekend, and now all I want to do is hack a recipe for the restaurant’s blini with dressed Ballycotton Bay crab. Or, at least, I want to make blini tonight and put something on top of them. The blini alone — buckwheat nutty and tangy with buttermilk — are superb. And what a canvas! If you don’t put fresh crab on them, then how about smoked salmon and crème fraîche, or smoked haddock with a little drained yogurt? Maybe just diced cucumber and crumbled marinated goat cheese? You’ll feel like a million dollars eating those.

    A NEW ALBUM

    Sara Bareilles, the Grammy-winning singer-songwriter who successfully detoured from the music industry to musical theater and then to television, released her first album in seven years last month. “Good Grief” is a song cycle in 14 tracks: plaintive, bristling piano ballads haunted by the deaths of close friends, including Gavin Creel, the beloved Broadway actor who died in 2024.

    Melena Ryzik, a culture reporter, profiled Bareilles. There are a lot — a lot — of tears.
