Good morning. After all these years, The Times found new developments in the O.J. Simpson case.

By Adam B. Kushner

I watched the infamous Ford Bronco chase from a hotel TV in Colorado.

O.J. Simpson said he didn’t do it. Then he authored a book titled “If I Did It,” in which he said he had help. He outlined a “hypothetical” in those pages, and it went like this: A friend was with him on the summer evening in 1994 when he murdered Nicole Brown Simpson and Ronald Goldman. That person disposed of the knife and Simpson’s bloody clothes.

It was a strange exercise, that 2007 book. But what if it wasn’t hypothetical? New reporting by The Times suggests that the assist may have actually happened — and identifies a possible accomplice.

A quick reminder: In 1995, a criminal jury found Simpson not guilty of murdering his ex-wife and her friend. (A civil jury later found him liable for the deaths.) Prosecutors never turned up a murder weapon.

Joey Ippolito reportedly knew where it was, our reporters Mike Baker and Tim Arango discovered.

The hidden man

Ippolito was like a character from an Elmore Leonard novel: a drug runner, a Mafioso, a frequent felon — and a friend of Al Cowlings, Simpson’s former teammate and the eventual pilot of the white Ford Bronco as Simpson fled from the cops.

He was also a prison escapee. When the law caught up with him (hiding under a toupee) in New York’s Little Italy in the summer of 1995, he offered information in exchange for a reduced sentence. Federal agents knew him as a trustworthy informant who’d helped take down mob figures. He told them a wild tale about his pal Cowlings, who he said knew the location of Simpson’s knife. Officials believed Ippolito and helped secure a light punishment for the jailbreak.

They also told Los Angeles prosecutors about what Ippolito had said. But by that point, Simpson’s trial was nearly over. Government lawyers had already rested their case and didn’t try to introduce Ippolito’s tips. (Cowlings did not respond to The Times’s many requests to comment on this narrative.)

Ippolito’s informant account was sealed. But he died in 2006. The Times asked a judge to make it public this summer, and she saw no reason not to.

Our reporters also tracked down Ippolito’s brother, Sam, who added a great deal to the yarn. Sam, who runs a motel in New Jersey, said Joey was with Simpson that night. He returned to the apartment the brothers shared with what appeared to be literal blood on his hands, bearing a bag that contained a bulky object, presumably the knife. In this version, which aligns with the scenario in Simpson’s book, Al Cowlings has no role and Joey Ippolito is the bag man.

Joey asked his brother to get rid of the bag, Sam recalled. You have to read Mike and Tim’s article to find out what Sam did next.

An American fixation

The O.J. Simpson saga is one of those moments, like the J.F.K. assassination or 9/11, seared into our collective memory with a specific time and place; if you are old enough, you probably remember where you were and what you were doing.

But unlike those other events, which began and ended, the murder of Nicole Brown Simpson and Ronald Goldman is officially still an unsolved, ongoing mystery. “This is probably the most scrutinized criminal case in American history,” Mike, an investigative reporter based in Seattle, told me:

So it was kind of shocking to me to find that there was still an untold part of the story, and that there were law enforcement records that people did not know about after all these years.

The new evidence won’t bring definitive closure. (Especially because Sam Ippolito recanted his story when Times reporters declined to pay him for it. The Times, like most news outlets, does not pay for tips.) Simpson will never face the new accusations: He died two years ago.

Yet for those transfixed by this whodunit — and the books, shows, documentaries and debates it has spawned — our new investigation offers several intriguing new puzzle pieces. Read it here.

    BOOK OF THE WEEK

    By Elisabeth Egan

    “Partita,” by Barbara Kingsolver

    Kingsolver’s follow-up to her Pulitzer Prize-winning novel “Demon Copperhead” begins, as many sagas do, with a blast from the past. Livia Cable is living quietly and, for the most part, happily on a dairy farm in Appalachia when she receives a call from the man who derailed her ambitions as a piano prodigy and scholarship student decades earlier. He wants to visit. As Livia decides how to answer, Kingsolver whisks readers back to Livia’s college days — her whirlwind romance, her lofty aspirations, her haunted family. Suddenly the question at the heart of this lyrical novel is less about Livia’s reunion with her old flame or how she ended up where she is but rather when — and whether — she’ll hear the music that infuses the world she’s in now.
