.DISCUSSION... Issued at 158 PM EDT Mon Oct 26 2026

Fairly quiet pattern continues across the area. Main concern for this shift has been the potential for fog overnight tonight into tomorrow morning. A west to east weak stationary boundary feature currently is in place over northern IN. Right now the current thinking is that the southwestern portions of the CWA have the better chance at fog as winds further north and east look to be a bit stronger keeping the lower levels better mixed and amount of moisture will also be lower in those locations which will help to keep fog development at bay. The other question is how much mid level clouds associated along the boundary develops which would also hinder radiational cooling. That is why there is a bit of uncertainty with this forecast in particular as any wavering of the boundary would change things. So will be keeping a close eye on things tonight.

Otherwise, dry conditions will primarily continue with a fairly zonal flow aloft in place through the remainder of the week. However, on Thursday a weak disturbance ripples through just to the north of the area and may bring light rain showers and even a rumble of thunder for areas mainly north of US-6 and especially for areas near and adjacent to Lake Michigan. Temperatures through the week look to remain above normal with highs in the 70s and lows generally in the upper 40s to mid 50s. Next week the longer range guidance does show the pattern in place now beginning to break down and becoming potentially more active. The 6-10 day outlook does show above average chances for precipitation and much above normal temperatures.

Download the app and get started reading our in-depth reporting, explainers, poll trackers and more. There’ll be no paywall until we know all the results.

There’s more below. But first, I’m going to turn to Jodi Rudoren, who once led our coverage of Israel, to talk to David Halbfinger, who runs it now. The subject of their conversation: tomorrow’s legislative elections in Israel. Those will determine if Benjamin Netanyahu remains the country’s prime minister.

By Jodi Rudoren

I covered Israeli elections in 2013 and 2015 while serving as Jerusalem bureau chief of The Times.

The most important election in Israel’s 78-year history is on Tuesday. It’s the first contest since the Oct. 7, 2023, Hamas-led terror attack that spawned three years of brutal war that destroyed the Gaza Strip and rippled across the Middle East.

Benjamin Netanyahu, already Israel’s longest-serving prime minister, is trying for his seventh term. Leading the opposition is Gadi Eisenkot, a former military chief whose son was killed while serving in Gaza.

Israel has a parliamentary system, so we may not know the real winner for weeks. Whoever does best on Tuesday then has to try to form a coalition government. Netanyahu would likely rely on ultra-Orthodox and extremist right-wing leaders, Eisenkot on centrist and center-right figures, as well as the left-wing Democrats. Arab-Israeli politicians could help tip the balance. It’s complicated, consequential and close, according to the polls.

I asked David Halbfinger, our Jerusalem bureau chief, what he is seeing and hearing.

People are calling this election existential. What’s at stake, and what are the top issues in the campaign?

It’s almost mind-boggling how many huge issues are hanging in the balance: whether to drift toward authoritarianism, whether all citizens should share in the sacrifice of defending the country, whether to ease up pressure on the Palestinians or end any thought of a two-state solution forever, and more. But I’d boil it down to a choice between two radically different visions for what kind of Jewish state Israel should be.

What are those two visions?

Simply put, should Israel be a Jewish-flavored, Western-style liberal democracy, or an Orthodox Jewish country that also holds elections but has more in common in some ways with Turkey or Hungary?

The campaign feels like a referendum on Netanyahu, who was Israel’s youngest premier when he was first elected, and now, at 77, would be the oldest if he wins. Why does he have so much staying power?

He’s simply the most gifted politician this country has ever produced. It says something that even while standing trial on corruption charges, even while saddled with unpopular and extremist coalition partners, even after allowing the Oct. 7 massacre on his watch, he could win another term.

What is Eisenkot’s appeal?

You know how roughly half or more of Israel has been clamoring for “anyone but Bibi”? Eisenkot is the un-Bibi. He could have been created in a lab: He’s the son of Moroccan immigrants. He grew up in working-class towns. His bootstraps rise began in the Golani Brigade, a classic military melting pot. And he earned a reputation for straight talk and candor, where Netanyahu is known for talking out of both sides of his mouth.

For several years, you ran The Times’s politics coverage. How are campaigns in Israel similar to those in the United States, and how are they different?

They’re incredibly short, just a few months long. The TV ads only come at the end, so the warfare has been chiefly through A.I.-generated social-media videos, some of them hilarious and many of them brutal. There have been very few big rallies, and even the big ones are tiny by American standards. Then again, this country is the size of New Jersey.

When I was posted there, I was struck by how much politics pulsed through casual conversation. What are the vibes around this election?

It’s gotten very, very dark. Everyone now has a grave fear, bordering on despair — that the country they thought they knew is on the verge of collapsing or morphing into something very different, perhaps even into the kind of place that they can’t see as a place to raise their children. More people have left Israel than immigrated to Israel in the last couple of years, and there’s a lot of talk about those departures accelerating depending on how this election turns out.

    RECIPE OF THE DAY

    I had dinner at Le Veau d’Or the other night, a little jewel box on East 60th Street that could be New York’s best French restaurant. (I’m saying it is.) You can think about a meal there for weeks. The food’s amazing, and I wish I could make some of it at home. But Veau is one of those restaurants where the cooking is so technical, precise and alchemical that even a confident home cook like me knows he’ll never in 2,000 years be able to do that.

    But one morning I woke up thinking about one of the appetizers the kitchen sends out: pommes soufflées with red caviar, crème fraîche and dill (above). Well. You can buy salmon roe, crème fraîche and dill at the store. Also those home-fry patties of the sort they sell at McDonald’s. Fry a bunch of those in shimmering oil, top them as elegantly as you can with the cream, caviar and herbs, and serve to applause. It’s a Temu version of fancy restaurant cooking, I know, but still very delicious.
