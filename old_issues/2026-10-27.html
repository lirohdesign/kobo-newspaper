02. nyt briefing

By Sam Sifton

Good morning. Israelis are voting today in a national election that many see as a referendum on Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu. Follow our live coverage here.

There’s more below. We also have six takeaways from a new memoir by the Washington Post reporter Bob Woodward. But first, let’s take a look at next week’s midterm elections in the United States.

Polling suggests Republicans may have a tough Tuesday next week as Americans vote in the midterm elections. That’s in part what brought President Trump to Mississippi, a state he won by about 23 points in 2024, to campaign last night for the incumbent G.O.P. senator.

It also fueled Vice President JD Vance’s travels yesterday, first to ruby-red Montana to help a House candidate and then to the reliable Republican stronghold of Kansas to stump for Senator Roger Marshall.

What’s going to happen next Tuesday? “This is as good a political environment as Democrats could ever wish,” the editor of the nonpartisan Cook Political Report told The Times, “and as bad as Republicans could ever imagine.”

But a so-called blue wave is by no measure assured. Some competitive Senate races could swing toward the Republicans as they rally their most loyal voters to the polls. Democrats could eke out wins in red states but fail to do so in bluer ones.

Everyone’s on edge. We’ll get the outcome the people who fill out the ballots provide. Polling ain’t voting.

Map quest

In some ways, it’s surprising to see so many races rated as tossups. Congressional districts are already often configured to make incumbents safe. At Trump’s insistence, Texas, Florida, North Carolina, Ohio, Tennessee, Alabama and Louisiana again redrew their maps to help Republicans in recent years. That effort — countered by a gerrymander in California to help Democrats — was meant to prevent the heavy midterm losses that a sitting president’s party historically experiences.

How odd are the new maps? My colleagues at The Upshot, journalists who specialize in analyzing data, used thousands of computer simulations to draw congressional maps in all 44 states with more than one district, this time without any consideration of partisanship or race.

They were looking for fairness: maps that resemble the simulations. Wisconsin’s map is pretty fair, for instance. It favors Republicans 6 to 2 over Democrats because, while half of Wisconsinites may vote for Democrats, they’re clustered in just two regions in the state.

The map in Texas, on the other hand, is far more brazenly drawn. You don’t even need the simulations to see how much the new map helps Republican chances. “On the north side of Austin, you can drive through five congressional districts in about 10 minutes,” my colleagues write. “In the suburbs of Dallas, there are voters drawn into districts that stretch hundreds of miles, including one to the New Mexico border.” These overwhelmingly benefit the right. Our party-blind simulations might have delivered a dozen additional competitive or Democratic-leaning districts in the state.

Tennessee provides a similar story. Republican lawmakers there cut up the Memphis and Nashville areas among multiple congressional districts and all but assured themselves a 9-to-0 map. That’s maybe no surprise in a vibrantly red state that Trump won by 30 points in 2024. But the “fair” map the simulations came up with drew separate districts for the two cities, both of which lean toward the Democrats.

And Illinois provides a terrific example of how redrawing a map may not provide as big of an advantage as desired. In 2021, state Democrats adopted a gerrymander with some truly awkward shapes — the 13th District there runs snakelike from East St. Louis through Springfield to Champaign, home of the University of Illinois. But The Times’s simulations suggest that the gains were modest. The neutral map the simulations created, for example, might replace the 13th with a more compact Republican-leaning district. But the simulations also show that Democrats might still be competitive in it.
