By Sam Sifton

Good morning. Binance, the world’s largest crypto exchange, said it had halted withdrawals after hackers stole more than $6 billion.

We have more news below. But let’s start in the nation’s capital, and then visit China to see the latest development in the war against mosquitoes. 🦟

The best view in D.C. is from the top of the Washington Monument, 555 feet above the National Mall. Spin in a circle and you can take the nickel tour: the White House, the Capitol, the Reflecting Pool, the Lincoln Memorial, the Jefferson Memorial, all the hits.

It’s a great place, in other words, to see President Trump’s imprint on the city, even as his projects face legal challenges. To grasp the extent of his Washingtonian makeover (and to allow you to experience the grandeur of the urban landscape), Times journalists built a high-resolution panorama, using 32 overlapping, close-up images our photographers captured at the top of the monument. Then software stitched them together into a 360-degree projection that acts like a custom-made Google Street View built from the perspective of a soaring bird.

It’s super cool. You can zoom in on the new helipad Trump installed outside the White House, and the new ballroom rising where the East Wing once stood. There’s the Eisenhower Executive Office Building he thinks is the wrong color. See where he wants to raise a 250-foot arch, and where he wants to replace athletic fields with a statue garden dedicated to American heroes. And there in the distance, you can see East Potomac Park, where Trump has already removed hundreds of trees as he replaces a municipal golf course with a championship-level one.

A number of federal judges have said that Trump can’t build this element or that one without the approval of Congress. But the Republicans who control Congress have not pushed back on him, which is allowing him to remake Washington just the way he wants it. It’s starting. Come on up and see for yourself.

Mosquito lasers!

Check this out: A company in China has invented a $1,000 A.I.-enabled brick that tracks mosquitoes in flight and burns their wings off with a laser. The United States may be ahead of China in developing A.I. models, but the device is an example of how quickly China is putting powerful computing into consumer products.

The technology also has military potential. As Keith Bradsher, who saw a demonstration, writes, “It’s not hard to imagine how a system that tracks and shoots down mosquitoes today could be adapted to target drones tomorrow.”

What could go wrong? A lot. A laser powerful enough to dispatch a mosquito is powerful enough to damage your eyes. To protect people (and animals that are not mosquitoes), A.I. software recognizes them and prevents the laser from shooting, even in darkness. A camera also identifies shiny surfaces so beams don’t ricochet elsewhere.

Keith’s description of the device’s effectiveness is bracing:

For anyone who has ever wished mosquitoes a horrible death, this is a strikingly high-tech way to make that wish a reality. The laser delivers a gruesome end, though not a quick one. Mosquitoes whose wings have burned off can take hours or even days to die, lying on the ground and twitching slightly.

After the demonstration, Keith examined the fallen bugs. Their wings were gone, but their legs and bodies appeared intact. They couldn’t fly, even when he waved his hand directly over them.

    • ASK THE MORNING

    I don’t understand how one party or the other can simply redraw the congressional district maps. Aren’t there rules for how and when this can be done? Who sets them? | Carol Roach | Overland Park, Kansas

    Yes, there are rules, but they vary by state. Typically, congressional districts are redrawn once a decade after the census to ensure they contain about the same number of people. But federal law allows more frequent redistricting. In states where the legislature controls the process (instead of an independent commission), a majority party can pass a new map. States can even change their rules when they want, something California voters did last fall. The Supreme Court also recently changed its interpretation of the Voting Rights Act, clearing the way for states to eliminate majority-minority districts by redrawing maps. — Eve Washington, a data journalist with The Upshot
