.DISCUSSION... Issued at 150 PM EST Tue Nov 3 2026

- Gusty conditions this afternoon with winds 25-35 mph.

Warm air advection driven by southerly flow has led to quite the warm day across the Michiana region and northwestern Ohio this afternoon with highs already in the low to mid 70s. Mostly clear skies combined with ample mixing may allow highs today to challenge the daily record of 76F. Regardless, it will feel more like late summer than November across the area. Increased mixing depth around peak heating this afternoon will also tap into an approaching LLJ with gusts as high as 35 mph, highest across Northwest Indiana into Southwest Michigan.

- Breezy conditions and rain showers expected Tuesday Night into Wednesday

Water vapor imagery shows a robust trough across the middle Mississippi Valley at present forecast to move into the region tonight into Wednesday. The surface reflection of this will be in the form of a cold front crossing the area during the day, with widespread showers out ahead of it. Expect rain much of the day Wednesday given the slow moving nature of the front as the surface low becomes vertically aligned with the parent trough. A dry slot moving in later Wednesday ends the rain showers, with northwest Ohio last to dry out in the evening. REFS probabilistic guidance favors less than 1/4" for most, with rainfall as high as 0.5" across our southeast Indiana counties into NW Ohio.

- Cooler with lake driven rain showers into the weekend

More robust cold air arrives with persistent troughing Friday into the weekend behind a cold frontal boundary. 850 mb temps fall to near zero, with lake temps still in the mid teens. This will be sufficient instability wise to generate lake effect rain showers Thursday Night into Friday. The trough and coldest mid-level air will depart to the east Saturday with northwest flow into early next week. How much if any precipitation we receive if any will depend on shortwaves embedded within the northwest flow as well as whether a ridge across the central CONUS drives flow be more zonal for a period early next week.

02. nyt briefing

By Sam Sifton

Good morning. It’s Election Day. See when the polls close where you are.

Yesterday, we told you how today’s midterm elections could play out, based on the latest polling. This morning, let’s look at what that might mean for the Senate.

(All our politics coverage is free to read, by the way, even if you’re not a subscriber. Follow our live updates all day.)

Whichever party wins, the Senate majority is likely to be narrow. Maybe neither party will have a majority!

Carl Hulse, our chief Washington correspondent, lays out the possibilities and the consequences. There are a number of them that could change how the Senate functions during the second half of President Trump’s term in office.

A slim majority empowers senators in the middle.

Democrats have 47 seats, and they’re likely to pick up more. With a majority, Democrats would make legislation impossible for the Republican leadership, but anything close to 50 could embolden Republicans who are willing to break with Trump and the party at large.

That’s Senators Lisa Murkowski of Alaska and Susan Collins of Maine (should Collins win her re-election race). A slim majority for the Republicans could also put pressure on a potential party-switcher like Senator John Fetterman, Democrat of Pennsylvania, who has quarreled with Democratic leadership but who has said that he does not intend to switch.

A 50-50 tie is bad news for JD Vance.

A split down the middle means a Republican Senate, given the vice president’s power to break deadlocks with a vote as the chamber’s “presiding officer.” But a tied Senate also leaves both parties in a situation where even a single defection could alter the outcome of a vote. And a single-member absence could make it impossible to move ahead with a bill. It would also mean that Vice President JD Vance, who is expected to be a leading Republican candidate for president in 2028, couldn’t leave Washington to campaign while the Senate is in session. He’d have to be nearby to break tie votes.

In that scenario, Democrats would likely lobby Murkowski to flip her party affiliation, though she’s unlikely to do so because she’d lose her powerful committee positions by changing sides. But it has happened before, Carl reminds. In 2001, Jim Jeffords, a Republican senator from Vermont, became an independent and started caucusing with the Democrats. That gave them a Senate majority.

Independents could scramble the picture.

There are two independents currently in the Senate: Bernie Sanders of Vermont and Angus King of Maine. Both caucus with Democrats. But at least three more are running, and they say they wouldn’t support the leadership of either party. The idea is, their independence would give them leverage over both parties.

You have to get into the way-back machine to see the last time that happened, Carl reports:

The Senate has not faced such a situation since 1952, when Senator Wayne Morse, Republican of Oregon, declared himself an independent, later moving a folding chair into the center aisle of the Senate. He was denied committee seats and ultimately joined the Democratic Party.

This could all take a while.

Challenges to election results could mean it takes time to seat new members. For example, Republicans may contest the results of the race in Alaska, where the Democrats recruited a candidate to siphon votes from the Republican incumbent. If the seat remains open during litigation, it would leave the balance of power in the Senate in question.

For how long? In 2008, it took more than eight months for Senator Al Franken, the Minnesota Democrat, to gain his seat amid a protracted recount and court battle.
