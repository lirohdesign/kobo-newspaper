02. nyt briefing

By Sam Sifton

Good morning. In a rebuke to President Trump, the Democrats flipped both the Senate and the House.

The midterms brought the blue wave that Democrats had hoped for and Republicans had feared. Democrats romped across the map as voters lamented high prices. They won right-leaning House districts, red-state governors’ races and Senate seats that haven’t been blue for generations.

It was a dramatic turnaround from just two years ago, when President Trump stormed back to the White House thanks to voter dissatisfaction about, yes, prices. With majorities in both houses of Congress, Republicans have run Washington since 2024. That is over — and it is the beginning of the end of the Trump era, my colleague Peter Baker writes.

Democrats originally feared yesterday’s election because so many of their senators in purple states were on the ballot. Instead, they performed beyond their expectations, winning races where they hadn’t even imagined they’d be competitive.

Trump said last night that he was “absolutely willing” to accept the results and was prepared to work with Democrats.

Here are the main takeaways. (And you can follow live results all day long. Our politics coverage is currently free to read in the app, even if you’re not a subscriber. Download it now.)

Democrats take both chambers

In the House, Democrats have won 228 seats so far, a majority that is expected to widen. The vote count is ongoing, but the party is on track to win the national House popular vote by 13 percentage points, according to our estimates. That would be the largest popular victory in the national congressional vote for either party in 50 years.

To retake the Senate, Democrats knew going into Election Day that they needed to flip four seats. It was clear by the evening that they’d hit their mark, as ordinarily safe Republican seats — like those in Texas and South Carolina — fell to Democrats.

It was a night of firsts

Winners include the first Muslim senator (Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan), the first Black female governor (Keisha Lance Bottoms of Georgia), the first openly gay male senator (Chris Pappas of New Hampshire), the first Hispanic governors elected in Texas (Gina Hinojosa) and California (Xavier Becerra), the first female governor of Ohio (Amy Acton), the first Black governors of Florida (Byron Donalds) and Wisconsin (David Crowley) and the first female Native American governor (Deb Haaland of New Mexico). If Democrats pick Hakeem Jeffries to lead them in the House, as they are expected to do, he’ll be the first Black speaker.

How Democrats won

High turnout shaped the outcome. In some places, turnout looked more like a presidential election. People likely cast about 10 million votes in Texas, a record for a midterm election there. Black voters and young voters swerved left. And across the country, counties with a majority of white working-class voters, a key component of Trump’s base, have swung about 14 points toward Democrats.

Gerrymandering didn’t work. Republicans hoped to pick up as many as 15 House seats with redrawn maps in seven states. But they’ve lost most of the ones that had been called by this morning as the electorate lurched leftward.

Money couldn’t save conservatives. Republicans outspent Democrats, and hundreds of millions of dollars flooded the political system. (These charts show how lopsided it was.) The huge spending advantage didn’t matter.

Unforced errors hurt Republicans. In Texas and South Carolina primaries, Trump backed Republicans for Senate who turned out to be weak general-election candidates. The president also threatened to back someone in a primary against North Carolina’s Republican senator, who retired and was replaced by a Democrat last night. Two otherwise safe Republican representatives fell because one (Tom Kean Jr. of New Jersey) vanished for several months and another (Max Miller of Ohio) was accused of abusing his ex-wife and daughter.

Expect investigations in Washington

Divided control of Washington will halt Trump’s legislative agenda. Democrats generally can’t pass laws without the president’s signature, but they plan to open investigations into many corners of his administration, such as the mass firings of his early months and the business deals that have enriched members of his family and his cabinet. With a Senate majority, Democrats can also block Trump’s judicial nominees, including for any possible Supreme Court vacancy.

How did Trump respond? He spoke to The Times and other journalists and sounded resigned. But he stressed his veto power in an interview with Fox News. He also posted online: “I wasn’t on the Ballot and if I was, we would have won IN A LANDSLIDE! Thank you for your attention to this matter.”

THE RESULTS

In the Senate

Texas: James Talarico, a state legislator and Christian seminarian, will become the state’s first Democratic senator in three decades. At 37, he’ll be the youngest member of the upper chamber. “We just showed that a politics of division is no match for a politics of love,” he said in his victory speech.

Michigan: Abdul El-Sayed, a Democrat, won in a bitter and expensive race against the Republican Mike Rogers. In his victory speech, he introduced himself using his full name — Abdulrahman Mohamed El-Sayed — and celebrated that “America is big enough for all of us.”

South Carolina: The Democrat Annie Andrews, a pediatrician, defeated Senator Darline Graham, the political novice whom Trump pushed to replace her late brother.

Kansas: Adam Hamilton, a megachurch pastor, became the first Democrat to win a Senate seat in the state in nearly 100 years, ousting Roger Marshall, a freshman senator.

Maine: Senator Susan Collins, New England’s sole Republican in Congress, lost her seat of three decades to Troy Jackson, a former Democratic state legislator.

Ohio: Former Senator Sherrod Brown will again represent his state after ousting Senator Jon Husted, who was appointed to replace JD Vance.

Iowa: Josh Turek, a Paralympian who is a Democratic state lawmaker, flipped an open Senate seat in a state Trump won three times.

Florida: Senator Ashley Moody, a Republican, fended off a tougher-than-expected challenge from a democratic socialist for the seat once held by Marco Rubio.

Mississippi: The Democratic candidate, Scott Colom, is leading by just a few votes. The state will take days to count provisional ballots that could decide the race.

Votes are still being tallied in Alaska. It could be a long wait.

In the House

Texas: Bobby Pulido, a moderate Democrat and Tejano musician, ousted a Republican — a sign of deep Latino voter frustration.

Iowa: A Democrat and minister, Sarah Trone Garriott, flipped a seat.

New York: Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez won her fifth term. And Representative Mike Lawler, a Republican, conceded to his Democratic opponent, Cait Conley. (The Times has yet to call the race.)

Virginia: A Democrat, Elaine Luria, won in a competitive district often seen as a national bellwether. (It has flipped party control five times since 2008.)

Florida: Democrats — including Pia Dandiya, a former high school principal — won districts that Republicans redrew in their favor.

Alabama: Representative Shomari Figures, a Democrat, held on to his seat despite Republican redistricting after the Supreme Court weakened the Voting Rights Act.

Governor races

Texas: In a tectonic upset, the Democrat Gina Hinojosa ousted the Republican governor, Greg Abbott, who was running for a fourth term. The decades of total Republican domination in Texas are over.

Ohio: After a bitter race, Amy Acton, a Democrat, defeated Vivek Ramaswamy. His attack ad featuring her mother divided Republicans.

California: A Democrat and former Biden administration official, Xavier Becerra, will succeed Gov. Gavin Newsom, who couldn’t run again because of term limits.

Nevada: Aaron Ford, a Democrat, defeated the incumbent Republican governor in a critical swing state.

Florida: Representative Byron Donalds maintained Republicans’ nearly three-decade hold on the governorship there.

Minnesota: Senator Amy Klobuchar, a Democrat, will succeed Gov. Tim Walz, who suspended his campaign for a third term amid a scandal over social services fraud.

Ballot measures

Abortion: Missouri will retain abortion rights after voters decided not to restore a ban. But in Idaho, voters rejected a measure to repeal the state’s abortion ban.

Gender: Nebraska voters approved a constitutional amendment that will require schools to designate sports teams as male, female or coeducational and prohibit male participation on female teams. Arizona voters approved a similar measure.

Foie gras: Washington, D.C., banned the fatty goose or duck liver delicacy.

Felons: Virginia restored the right of people convicted of felonies to vote after release from prison.

Billionaires: It’s too soon to say whether California passed a one-time wealth tax on the superrich.

Firearms: Idaho picked a state gun.
